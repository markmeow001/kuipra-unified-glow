We see it constantly in Vancouver: a business pours weeks of budget and effort into an event — a launch, an open house, a pop-up, an anniversary dinner — and its entire social media footprint is one announcement post and a handful of blurry phone photos posted the morning after.

The event was real. The marketing barely happened.

The fix isn't spending more. It's a mental shift: **stop treating an event as a date and start treating it as a campaign.** A date produces one day of content. A campaign has a before, a during, and an after — which is why the same event, covered properly, gives you roughly three times as much to post. That's our rule of thumb, not a measured figure.

Here's the three-phase framework we run for our own client events.

## Phase 1 — Before: build anticipation, not just awareness

Pre-event content has one job: make cold audiences care before they've ever heard of you. An announcement alone doesn't do that. A sequence does. For most business events, three to four weeks of runway is enough; larger ticketed events need six to eight.

### The pre-event sequence

- **Teasers.** Reveal the event in pieces — the venue, the theme, a behind-the-scenes glimpse of preparation. Curiosity outperforms information at this stage. A useful filter comes from [Science World's long-running ads](/blog/science-world-advertising-teardown/): each piece has to be true, short, and make people want to know more.
- **Speaker and guest spotlights.** Every speaker, performer, or featured guest is a piece of content *and* a distribution channel. Spotlight them individually, tag them, and most will reshare to their own audience.
- **Countdown posts.** "Two weeks out," "three days left" — simple, but they create urgency and give fence-sitters a reason to decide.
- **Partner cross-promotion.** Sponsors, vendors, and the venue all have audiences that overlap with yours but aren't identical. Agree on a shared posting schedule so each partner introduces the event to a fresh pool of people. On Instagram, a [Collab post](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/08/music-and-collabs-on-instagram/) does this in one move: once a partner accepts, the same post appears on both profiles and reaches both audiences. Our guide to [local partnerships and sponsorships](/blog/local-partnerships-sponsorship-marketing/) covers how to pick those partners and put the deal in writing.
- **Creator invites.** Local creators on the guest list extend your reach, but a free invitation is still a material connection they should disclose. Ad Standards' fall 2025 guidelines suggest #InvitedGuest when a creator was simply invited, and clearer wording if you covered travel or a hotel. Our [influencer marketing guide](/blog/vancouver-influencer-marketing-guide/) has the rest of the rules.

Check the date against the [Vancouver seasonal marketing calendar](/blog/vancouver-seasonal-marketing-calendar/) before you announce it. An open house the same week as Lunar New Year or Black Friday is competing for attention it won't get.

This phase is where the funnel actually fills. People discovering you through a speaker spotlight or a partner's story are entering at the top — and by event day, they're no longer cold.

## Phase 2 — During: coverage is a job, not an afterthought

The most common live-coverage failure isn't lack of effort. It's improvisation. Someone remembers mid-event to grab their phone, and the results look like it.

Live coverage works when it's planned like everything else:

- **Write a shot list before doors open.** Arrivals, setup details, host close-ups, crowd reactions, product moments. If it's not on the list, it usually doesn't get shot.
- **Assign content roles.** One person captures, one person posts. When the same person does both, both suffer.
- **Post stories through the day.** Real-time stories reward followers who couldn't attend and signal momentum to everyone watching.
- **Bank reel footage for later.** Not everything captured today gets posted today. Short vertical clips — energy, laughter, the room filling up — are raw material for Phase 3.
- **Hit the peak moments.** The ribbon cut, the toast, the reveal. These are scheduled; being ready for them is a choice, not luck.
- **Tell guests you're filming.** A small sign at the entrance does it. Before you feature someone's face up close in a post, a quick "okay if we share this?" saves an awkward takedown later.

## Phase 3 — After: this is where the compounding happens

Most businesses go silent the day after an event. That silence wastes the phase with the highest return, because post-event content reaches the largest audience: everyone who *didn't* attend.

- **Recap edits.** A 30–60 second cut of the banked footage, published within the first few days while the event is still fresh. Our [short-form video guide](/blog/short-form-video-guide-vancouver/) covers hooks and formats that work for this.
- **Attendee-generated content.** Reshare what guests posted, with credit. It's the most credible proof your event was worth attending. Instagram added a native repost button in August 2025 that works on public posts and [credits the original poster](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/08/new-instagram-features-help-you-connect/); for your own feed, website or ads, ask first.
- **Highlight narratives over the following weeks.** One story per week — a speaker's best moment, a behind-the-scenes thread, a "what we learned" post. Each one re-exposes the event to people who missed the earlier coverage.
- **Warm the audience for the next one.** End the cycle with a bridge: "Want in next time?" A well-run Phase 3 is quietly Phase 1 of your next event. Run that loop for a few years and the event can become the brand's community, the way [Fluevog's Flummunity Fest](/blog/fluevog-community-teardown/) gives the shoe brand's fans a reason to travel.

## The copy-paste checklist

- [ ] Announcement + 2–3 teasers scheduled
- [ ] One spotlight post per speaker/guest, with tags
- [ ] Countdown posts at 2 weeks, 1 week, 3 days
- [ ] Partner cross-promotion schedule agreed
- [ ] Creator invitations sent, with disclosure expectations in writing
- [ ] Shot list written; capture and posting roles assigned
- [ ] Stories posted live at peak moments
- [ ] Vertical reel footage banked
- [ ] Recap edit published within 3 days
- [ ] Attendee content reshared with credit
- [ ] One highlight post per week for 3–4 weeks
- [ ] Bridge post teasing the next event

## The takeaway

An event is one of the few marketing investments that naturally produces weeks of authentic content — but only if you plan all three phases before the doors open.
