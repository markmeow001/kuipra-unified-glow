Metro Vancouver is one of the most Chinese-influenced metropolitan markets outside Asia. In the 2021 Census, 512,260 people in the Vancouver census metropolitan area identified as Chinese, 19.6% of the population, or about one in five residents ([Statistics Canada, Vancouver CMA profile](https://www12.statcan.gc.ca/census-recensement/2021/dp-pd/prof/details/page.cfm?Lang=E&DGUIDlist=2021S0503933&GENDERlist=1&STATISTIClist=1,4&HEADERlist=0)). In Richmond the share was 54.3%, or 113,060 people ([Statistics Canada, Richmond profile](https://www12.statcan.gc.ca/census-recensement/2021/dp-pd/prof/details/page.cfm?Lang=E&DGUIDlist=2021A00055915015&GENDERlist=1&STATISTIClist=1,4&HEADERlist=0)). For many local businesses, this isn't a "niche segment"; it's a major share of their addressable market.

Yet most Vancouver marketing plans treat the Chinese community as an afterthought: translate the flyer, maybe boost a post around Lunar New Year, done. Here's what actually works, starting with what the numbers do and don't tell you.

## What the census actually says

These are Statistics Canada's 2021 Census Profile figures for the Vancouver CMA ([source](https://www12.statcan.gc.ca/census-recensement/2021/dp-pd/prof/details/page.cfm?Lang=E&DGUIDlist=2021S0503933&GENDERlist=1&STATISTIClist=1,4&HEADERlist=0)), still the most recent census data:

- **Identify as Chinese** (the "visible minority" population group): 512,260 people, 19.6%
- **Report Chinese ethnic or cultural origin:** 475,160 people, 18.2%
- **Mother tongue:** Mandarin 191,475 (7.3%), Cantonese 182,910 (7.0%), "Chinese, n.o.s." 2,125 (0.1%)
- **Language spoken most often at home:** Mandarin 152,805 (5.8%), Cantonese 131,660 (5.0%), "Chinese, n.o.s." 1,275 (under 0.1%)

A few notes on reading these. The first two figures are shares of the 2,607,010 people in private households; the language figures are shares of 2,619,420 residents (institutional residents excluded) and count single responses only, so someone who reported Cantonese and English equally is not in them. "Chinese, n.o.s." (not otherwise specified) covers people who wrote "Chinese" without naming a language, and it is tiny: almost everyone names a specific language, mostly Mandarin or Cantonese. Statistics Canada now uses the term "racialized groups" in its analysis, but the profile table still labels this variable "visible minority".

Two things in these numbers matter for marketers. First, Mandarin and Cantonese are close to even region-wide, so neither is a safe default for audio and video. Second, geography changes the picture. Counting the language spoken most often at home, Cantonese clearly leads in the City of Vancouver (54,920 vs. 30,925 for Mandarin; [source](https://www12.statcan.gc.ca/census-recensement/2021/dp-pd/prof/details/page.cfm?Lang=E&DGUIDlist=2021A00055915022&GENDERlist=1&STATISTIClist=1,4&HEADERlist=0)), Burnaby leans Mandarin (25,565 vs. 19,090; [source](https://www12.statcan.gc.ca/census-recensement/2021/dp-pd/prof/details/page.cfm?Lang=E&DGUIDlist=2021A00055915025&GENDERlist=1&STATISTIClist=1,4&HEADERlist=0)) and Richmond is close to a tie (Mandarin 37,000, Cantonese 36,515). The census doesn't ask which script people read, so the Simplified vs. Traditional decision still has to come from knowing your segment.

Newer counts are coming. Statistics Canada ran the 2026 Census this year and has scheduled its language results for September 8, 2027 and its ethnocultural results for October 27, 2027 ([2026 Census release schedule](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/sites/default/files/documents/2026-09/census-2026-key-content-release-schedule.pdf)). Until then, 2021 is the latest official picture.

## First: it's not one market

The biggest strategic error is treating "the Chinese community" as a single audience. In practice there are at least three distinct segments, and they barely overlap in media habits:

1. **Recent immigrants and international students.** Mandarin-first, digitally native to the Chinese app ecosystem — WeChat for community and commerce, Xiaohongshu (RED) for discovery and reviews, Douyin for entertainment.
2. **Established immigrants** (10+ years). Often bilingual, mixing WeChat groups with Facebook, Instagram, and local Chinese-language media. Hong Kong-heritage households read Traditional Chinese; many mainland-heritage households read Simplified.
3. **Canadian-born Chinese (CBC).** English-first, culturally bilingual, reachable on the same platforms as any local audience — but responsive to brands that show genuine cultural fluency rather than festival-only pandering.

A campaign built for one of these segments will simply not reach the other two.

## The platform map

- **WeChat** — still the backbone for segment 1 and much of segment 2: group chats, Official Accounts, and word-of-mouth referrals. B2C service businesses (real estate, education, health, food) live and die by WeChat reputation. Our [WeChat guide](/blog/wechat-marketing-canadian-business/) explains how the ecosystem fits together, and the [WeChat private traffic playbook](/blog/wechat-private-traffic-playbook/) covers keeping customers once they add you.
- **Xiaohongshu (RED)** — the discovery engine. Restaurant openings, beauty, retail, and experiences get found here first. Reviews read as peer recommendations, which makes seeding authentic content far more effective than ads. Our [Xiaohongshu marketing guide](/blog/xiaohongshu-marketing-vancouver/) covers whether to run it in-house or hire help.
- **Instagram / Facebook** — the bridge platforms reaching segments 2 and 3, and where bilingual creative earns disproportionate attention because so few brands do it well. Whether to mix both languages in one account or run two is its own decision; see [Bilingual Brands: One Social Account or Two?](/blog/bilingual-social-media-account-strategy/)
- **Local Chinese media & community groups** — still surprisingly effective for older demographics and high-trust categories.

## What good execution looks like

**Language with intent.** Choose Simplified vs. Traditional based on the segment, not convenience. Better yet, produce both — the cost is small and the signal of respect is large. For video, decide Mandarin vs. Cantonese by neighbourhood using the census split above, and subtitle in written Chinese.

**Cultural adaptation, not translation.** Lunar New Year is table stakes. Brands that also show up for Mid-Autumn Festival, understand gift-giving norms, use culturally resonant visuals, and reference local community touchpoints (yes, including specific Richmond food courts) read as insiders. For a large-scale example, our [T&T Supermarket teardown](/blog/tt-supermarket-bilingual-retail-teardown/) shows how a grocer serves Chinese-speaking and non-Chinese shoppers in the same store without translating one journey into the other.

**Community credibility before reach.** In WeChat and RED ecosystems, trust flows through people: group admins, micro-influencers, satisfied customers who post. Twenty genuine posts from real customers and local creators often outperform one big ad buy. If you gave anything in return, free product included, the post has to say so, in the language of the post; our [influencer marketing guide](/blog/vancouver-influencer-marketing-guide/) covers Canada's disclosure rules.

**Measure separately.** If your Chinese-market campaign is reported inside overall social metrics, you can't see what's working. Track it as its own funnel.
