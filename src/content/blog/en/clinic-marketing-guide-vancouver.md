Clinic marketing has a unique constraint no restaurant or salon deals with: you're marketing a regulated health service, under a college that has opinions about your ads — and under patients' most trust-sensitive purchase decision. Get it right and marketing compounds for years. Get it wrong and you're explaining a complaint to your college.

This guide covers the playbook for Vancouver dental, TCM, physio, chiro and aesthetics practices — including where the lines are. We updated it in November 2026, after BC's health colleges moved to new legislation and several published new advertising standards.

## First, know your lines

Every regulated health profession in BC answers to a college. On April 1, 2026, the Health Professions and Occupations Act replaced the old Health Professions Act for all of them ([BC Health Regulators](https://bchealthregulators.ca/health-professions-and-occupations-act-hpoa-now-in-force/)), and several colleges issued new standards to match. They share a core but are not identical. Here is what the four colleges covering most clinics in this guide say, as we read their current documents in November 2026:

- **Physicians, including doctor-led aesthetics (CPSBC).** The [Advertising and Communication with the Public](https://www.cpsbc.ca/files/pdf/CPSBC-PS-Advertising-Communication.pdf) standard (revised June 2026) rules out inducements such as gifts, discounts or time-limited offers for medical services; allows before-and-after photos only with the patient's written consent; bars comparisons with other providers and guarantees about results; and requires ads to say clearly which services aren't covered by MSP. It also states that promotion of medical services "is generally considered incompatible with a fiduciary relationship."
- **Dentists and the oral health team (BCCOHP).** The [Standards for the Oral Health Team](https://oralhealthbc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Standards-for-the-Oral-Health-Team.pdf) (April 2026) require marketing to be accurate and not misleading, and to comply with advertising, competition and privacy law. We didn't find a testimonial-specific rule in that document, so ask the college before using patient reviews or stories in your own ads.
- **Chiropractors, massage therapists, naturopathic physicians, TCM practitioners and acupuncturists (CCHPBC).** The [Advertising and Marketing standard](https://cchpbc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Pro.-Standard-Advertising-and-Marketing-FINALV2.pdf) says licensees must "avoid soliciting testimonials," and the college's [FAQ](https://cchpbc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/FAQAdverting.Marketing.V3.FINAL_.pdf) spells out that, from April 1, 2026, this includes asking a patient to leave a review or giving them a link or prompt to do so. The standard also rules out "expert" and "specialist" claims, comparative superiority and pressure-based tactics.
- **Physiotherapists, occupational therapists, dietitians, psychologists and the other CHCPBC professions.** The [Marketing, Advertising and Promotion standard](https://chcpbc.org/standards-and-resources/ethics-and-practice-standards/marketing-advertising-and-promotion/) defines testimonials to include reviews, requires licensees to monitor third-party content such as online reviews and directory listings and to request corrections where it's inaccurate, and bars unsubstantiated superiority claims and guaranteed results.

The pattern across all four: factual, verifiable, no superlatives, no promises. And the clinic is on the hook for its vendors. CPSBC and CHCPBC both say the licensee is responsible for marketing an agency prepares on their behalf, and expect that to be secured by contract.

None of this prevents effective marketing — it *shapes* it toward education and verifiable fact, which happens to be what patients trust anyway. Treat the summary above as a starting point, not legal advice: standards change, and the college that licenses the person delivering the treatment is the one that counts (for a nurse injector, that's the BC College of Nurses and Midwives). If a marketing vendor promises tactics that ignore any of this, that's your cue to leave. (It's one of the filter questions in our [choosing an agency guide](/blog/how-to-choose-marketing-agency-vancouver/).)

## The channel that matters: search and Maps

Patients pick clinics like this: search "dentist near me" or a symptom, scan the map pack, compare reviews and distance, check insurance and direct billing, book. Every step happens before you know they exist. So:

- **Google Business Profile, complete and precise** — exact category, services listed, insurance/direct-billing noted, booking link live, real photos of the space (patients are checking whether it looks clean and modern). Full setup in our [GBP guide](/blog/google-business-profile-guide-vancouver/). Keep the same name, address and hours on Bing, Apple Maps and health directories too; CHCPBC now expects licensees to monitor directory listings, and our [citations guide](/blog/local-business-citations-guide/) shows how to audit them.
- **Reviews, by your college's rules first.** If you're licensed by CCHPBC, don't ask at all: no review cards, no QR codes, no follow-up texts. Reviews patients post entirely on their own aren't treated as solicitation. Everyone else still has Google's 2026 rules to follow — no incentives, no asking only the patients you expect to be happy, no pressure in the clinic, no requested content — which our [Google reviews guide](/blog/google-reviews-management-guide/) walks through. Every response stays privacy-safe ("Thank you — we're glad to hear this" beats anything that confirms someone was a patient).
- **A website that answers the deciding questions** on the homepage: services, languages spoken, direct billing, parking, how to book. If patients call to ask it, it belongs on the page — that's the core of our [12-point conversion audit](/blog/website-conversion-audit-checklist/).

## Education content: the compliant growth engine

The highest-ROI clinic content is answering real patient questions in plain language: "Does a deep cleaning hurt?" "What does cupping actually do?" "How many physio sessions does a sprain need?" This works triple duty — it ranks for the searches patients actually make, it pre-builds trust before the first visit, and it's the content format every college we've reviewed is comfortable with: educational, factual, no promises. CHCPBC's standard adds a requirement worth borrowing whatever your profession: health information in marketing must be consistent with current, evidence-informed practice.

One well-written answer per week beats an expensive campaign per quarter. And in the AI era it compounds further — clear question-and-answer content on your own site is exactly what [AI assistants cite when recommending local providers](/blog/how-ai-assistants-recommend-local-businesses/).

One thing not to rely on: the Q&A box on your Google Business Profile. Google confirmed in December 2025 that it is removing Q&A from Google Maps in favour of an AI "Ask" feature ([Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-maps-qa-feature-ask-40594.html)), which early-2026 reports placed only in the US and India ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/google-q-and-a/)). The questions patients used to ask there — parking, languages, direct billing, whether you take new patients — belong on your website and in your profile's services and description. Accountants and lawyers work under the same kind of rules, and our [professional services content guide](/blog/professional-services-content-marketing-vancouver/) shows the same education-first approach from their side.

## The bilingual trust factor

For Vancouver clinics, language is a clinical-trust feature, not a marketing nicety. "國語/粵語服務" on your profile and site is a deciding factor for a large share of Richmond and Vancouver patients — and for TCM especially, Chinese-language content on WeChat and Xiaohongshu reaches patients researching in their own language, where health decisions actually get discussed. The same rule applies as everywhere: [translated captions aren't localized presence](/blog/marketing-to-vancouver-chinese-community/) — the clinic that answers questions natively in Chinese wins the referral group chat.

One caution in our reading: a patient's Xiaohongshu note about their treatment is a review in all but name, so your college's rules on soliciting testimonials apply there too. Educational notes are the safe format. Our [Xiaohongshu guide](/blog/xiaohongshu-marketing-vancouver/) covers running the account itself.

## Retention: recall is marketing too

A dental practice's revenue lives in hygiene recall; physio in treatment plans; aesthetics in maintenance cycles. Automated, personal-feeling recall messages (due-date reminders, seasonal checkup pushes, direct-billing renewal notes) cost almost nothing and out-earn any acquisition campaign. The [email principles](/blog/email-marketing-for-local-businesses/) apply — with the added rule that health communication must stay privacy-safe: no diagnosis details in a subject line, ever.

Promotional pushes also fall under Canada's anti-spam law (CASL), which covers instant messaging accounts as well as email and text. You need consent: a patient who paid for a service in the last two years gives you implied consent, and every message must identify the clinic and offer an unsubscribe ([CASL](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/page-2.html)). If many of your patients prefer WeChat, our [WeChat private traffic playbook](/blog/wechat-private-traffic-playbook/) covers running recall there within those rules.

## What we'd skip

Aggressive discounting: for physicians, CPSBC's standard rules out discounts and time-limited offers for medical services, and CCHPBC's FAQ lists discounts, free items and referral bonuses as inducements that can create a conflict of interest. Even where a discount is allowed, it attracts price-shoppers. Buying followers or engagement anywhere. Superlative-heavy ads a college could read as comparative claims. And any vendor who doesn't ask which college regulates you before proposing tactics.
