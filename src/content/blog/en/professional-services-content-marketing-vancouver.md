A customer can taste whether a restaurant is good. A patient can at least tell whether a dentist was gentle. But a client usually cannot tell whether their accountant structured something well, whether their lawyer's advice was the best available, or whether a consultant's recommendation was right — not before hiring, and often not after.

That is what makes professional services different to market. You are not selling an outcome the client can inspect. You are selling judgment the client has to trust.

This guide is for accountants, lawyers, bookkeepers, financial planners, immigration and business consultants, and similar firms in Metro Vancouver. The principles are the same; the regulator rules differ by profession, and we will flag where.

## Clients choose on signals, so give them better ones

When quality is hard to evaluate, people fall back on proxies: who referred them, how established you look, what reviews say, and — increasingly — whether you explained something clearly before they ever spoke to you.

That last proxy is the one you control completely.

A plain-language explanation of what happens when a small business misses a filing deadline, or how a lease review actually works, does two things. It helps the reader with their real problem. And it demonstrates, without any claims, that you think clearly and can explain complicated things simply — which is most of what a client wants from a professional.

This is the professional-services version of a principle we keep returning to: [specific, checkable content beats adjectives](/blog/arcteryx-technical-credibility-teardown/). "Experienced, trusted advisors" is a claim. A clear answer to a hard question is evidence.

## Know the boundaries first

Regulated professionals do not market with a blank page, and it is better to design your content around the rules than to retrofit it after a complaint.

**Lawyers in BC** are governed by the Law Society of British Columbia's Code of Professional Conduct. Its definition of marketing activity is broad: advertisements, promotional material, letterhead, business cards, **directory listings**, public appearances and any other way legal services are promoted. Rule 4.2-5 says marketing must not be false, inaccurate, **unverifiable**, reasonably capable of misleading, or contrary to the best interests of the public. The commentary adds two lines worth taping to the wall: marketing must not take advantage of someone's physical or emotional vulnerability, and it must not create an **unjustified expectation about the results** a lawyer can achieve.

**Accountants** registered with CPABC are governed by the CPABC Code of Professional Conduct, which includes provisions on advertising and soliciting work. Read the current Code — or ask CPABC's practice advisors — before running anything involving comparisons, result claims or client endorsements.

**Unregulated consultants** have more latitude, but the same standard is simply good practice: clients who are careful enough to hire a professional are careful enough to notice exaggeration.

Notice what these rules push you toward. "Unverifiable" and "unjustified expectation about results" rule out exactly the marketing that performs worst with thoughtful clients anyway — superlatives, guarantees, and implied outcomes. We saw the same dynamic in the [clinic marketing guide](/blog/clinic-marketing-guide-vancouver/): regulation doesn't stop effective marketing; it shapes it toward education and verifiable fact.

## The content that actually earns calls

Start with the questions you already answer in first meetings. Every firm has them, and they are almost always the same ten.

- **Process questions.** What happens first, what documents are needed, how long it takes.
- **Cost-driver questions.** Not a price list, but what makes a matter simpler or more complex, so people understand why quotes differ.
- **Deadline and consequence questions.** What happens if something is filed late, missed, or done incorrectly.
- **"Do I even need a professional?" questions.** Answering this honestly — including when the answer is no — builds more trust than almost anything else you can publish.
- **Situation questions.** Buying a first rental property, incorporating, bringing a family member over, closing a small business.

Give each substantial question its own page with a clear, direct answer near the top, then the detail. That structure helps a reader skim, and clear question-and-answer content is precisely the kind [AI assistants tend to cite](/blog/how-ai-assistants-recommend-local-businesses/) when someone asks for help in your area.

Seasonality matters in some professions. Accountants have an obvious peak ahead of the spring personal tax filing deadline; the content that wins that season gets published well before it, not during the rush when nobody has time to write.

## Referrals are still the main channel — content feeds them

For most professional firms, the largest source of new clients is referral: existing clients, other professionals, and community networks.

Content does not replace that. It makes referrals convert. When someone is told "you should talk to her," the next step is almost always a search. What they find in those first minutes either confirms the recommendation or quietly undermines it.

Three places to make that moment work:

- **Your website**, with the question pages above, clear service descriptions, and an obvious next step.
- **Your listings** — Google, Bing, Apple Maps and the relevant professional directories — consistent and complete. In BC law, directory listings count as marketing activity under the Code, so hold them to the same standard. The [citation map guide](/blog/local-business-citations-guide/) covers the rest.
- **Email to existing clients** — a short, genuinely useful note ahead of relevant deadlines or rule changes keeps you top of mind when they are asked who they use. The [email marketing guide](/blog/email-marketing-for-local-businesses/) covers doing it without becoming noise.

## Bilingual is a trust feature, not a translation task

A large share of Metro Vancouver residents research professional decisions in Chinese, Punjabi, Korean and other languages, especially for high-stakes matters involving money, property, family and immigration.

For those clients, being able to read a clear explanation in their own language is itself a trust signal. But it has to be native content written for that audience's actual questions, not translated English pages. The concerns of a newly arrived family setting up a business are different from those of a long-established local owner, and the content should reflect that. We cover how to run this properly in the [bilingual social account guide](/blog/bilingual-social-media-account-strategy/) and the [Chinese community marketing guide](/blog/marketing-to-vancouver-chinese-community/).

For regulated professionals, the same accuracy rules apply in every language. A translated claim that would be misleading in English is misleading in Chinese too.

## What not to do

- **Promise or imply outcomes.** "We always win," "maximum refund guaranteed," or anything that sets an unjustified expectation.
- **Use superlatives nobody can verify.** "Vancouver's best tax accountant" is unverifiable by definition.
- **Tell client stories that reveal confidential details.** Anonymizing a name is not enough if the situation is recognizable.
- **Publish thin content for the sake of volume.** Ten generic posts that could appear on any firm's site do less than one clear answer to a real question.
- **Ignore the lag.** Professional content compounds slowly. Firms that stop after three months never see the part that pays.

## Three moves to start with

**List your ten first-meeting questions** and publish clear answers to them, one page each.

**Audit your claims** against your regulator's rules: remove anything unverifiable or anything that implies a result.

**Make referrals land.** Check what someone would find in the first five minutes after hearing your name — website, listings, reviews — and fix the gaps.

The firms that win here are rarely the loudest. They are the ones whose answers a client has already read, understood and trusted before picking up the phone.
