Ask ten agencies where a small business should start with local SEO and you'll get ten different invoices. The honest answer is simpler than most of them want it to be: local search rewards a short list of fundamentals, done in the right order. Here's that order — and the expensive detours to avoid.

## Two result types, two different games

Search "emergency plumber Vancouver" and you'll see two distinct blocks. The **map pack** — three businesses pinned on a map — sits near the top for searches with local intent. Below it run the **organic results**: regular web pages.

These are ranked by different signals. The map pack draws heavily on your Google Business Profile and physical location; organic results depend on your website's content and authority. A business can dominate one and be invisible in the other. Most small businesses should chase the map pack first: it's more visible, more tappable on mobile, and more winnable without a big content budget.

## What AI search changes, and what doesn't

AI answers are now a third block on the page, and they get a lot of attention. For local searches, the map pack is still the main event:

- **AI Overviews mostly show up on informational searches.** In a 2025 Whitespark study of 540 manually collected queries across three US cities, AI Overviews appeared on 15% of local-intent queries, while local packs appeared on 93%. The two had what the authors called "an inverse relationship, based on query intent" ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/blog/case-study-the-prevalence-of-ai-overviews-in-local-search/)).
- **Google is testing AI-powered local packs, and they show fewer businesses.** Sterling Sky found these new packs (so far seen only in the US, on mobile) surfaced about 32% as many unique businesses as traditional 3-packs in its client data ([Sterling Sky](https://www.sterlingsky.ca/the-state-of-local-seo-in-2026/)). They hadn't reached Canada at the time of writing, but if they do, there will be fewer slots, and the fundamentals below matter more, not less.
- **People still check the map.** In a July 2026 BrightLocal survey of US consumers, 75% of people who used AI to find a local business checked Google or Maps before contacting it ([eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/shoppers-use-ai-find-local-businesses--then-double-check-on-google)).

## The three pillars Google actually uses

Google is unusually transparent about local ranking. Three factors decide it:

1. **Relevance** — how well your listing and site match what was searched. You control this: complete profile, accurate categories, pages that describe what you actually do.
2. **Distance** — how far you are from the searcher. You mostly can't control this, which is precisely why neighbourhood-level thinking matters (more below).
3. **Prominence** — how well-known and well-regarded you are: reviews, mentions across the web, links from local sites.

Every tactic worth paying for maps to one of these three. If a proposal can't tell you which pillar it moves, it moves none of them. For a sense of weighting: in Whitespark's 2026 survey of 47 local SEO practitioners, Google Business Profile signals were rated the biggest factor for map-pack rankings (32%), with reviews next (20%) ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/)). That is expert opinion, not a measured formula, but it matches what we see.

## The priority order

**1. Google Business Profile — before anything else.** It's free, it feeds the map pack directly, and most small business profiles are half-finished: missing categories, no services listed, three photos from 2019. Claim it, complete every field, choose categories deliberately, and post updates. We've written a full walkthrough in our [Google Business Profile guide for Vancouver businesses](/blog/google-business-profile-guide-vancouver/). Then set up [Bing Places](/blog/bing-places-setup-guide/) too; it takes one sitting and covers the searches Google doesn't.

**2. Consistent NAP citations.** Your name, address, and phone number should be identical everywhere they appear — Google, Yelp, the BBB, industry directories, your own footer. Inconsistencies ("4th Ave" here, "Fourth Avenue" there, an old phone number on a forgotten listing) erode Google's confidence in your data. A few dozen accurate citations beat hundreds of sloppy ones. Our [citation map guide](/blog/local-business-citations-guide/) shows which listings matter most.

**3. Location-specific website pages.** Your site is what backs up the map listing and what wins organic results. One clear page per core service, plus pages for the areas you serve, each with genuinely local content. And make sure those pages turn visitors into enquiries; our [website conversion audit](/blog/website-conversion-audit-checklist/) is a 12-point check.

**4. Reviews — steady, not bursty.** Reviews drive prominence and conversion at the same time. Build the ask into your process (after the job, in the follow-up email) rather than begging in occasional panics. Reply to every review, especially the bad ones — replies are read by future customers, not past ones. Google tightened its review rules in 2026 (no incentives, no pressure, no asking for specific content); our [Google reviews guide](/blog/google-reviews-management-guide/) covers how to build reviews within them.

**5. Local backlinks.** Links from Vancouver sources — neighbourhood BIAs, local news, community sponsorships, suppliers — signal prominence in a way generic directories can't. This is slow work. It's also the layer competitors are least likely to bother with. One caveat: Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) require links you pay for, including sponsorship links, to be marked `rel="sponsored"` or `nofollow`, so a sponsorship's value is the mention and the visitors it sends, not ranking power. Our [local partnerships guide](/blog/local-partnerships-sponsorship-marketing/) covers how to structure those deals.

## Think in neighbourhoods, not in "Vancouver"

"Vancouver" as a keyword is crowded and vague. But people don't search the way head-office keyword tools assume: they search "physio Kitsilano", "brunch Mount Pleasant", "tutoring Richmond". These neighbourhood queries have less competition, clearer intent, and searchers who are closer to buying — and closer to you, which helps the distance pillar do its job.

This is also where Metro Vancouver's demographics matter. If you serve Richmond, part of your local audience isn't searching in English at all — see our guide to [marketing to Metro Vancouver's Chinese community](/blog/marketing-to-vancouver-chinese-community/).

## Service-area pages beat one generic page

The lazy version is a single page listing every suburb from Squamish to White Rock. It ranks for none of them, because it's specific to none of them.

A real service-area page for, say, Mount Pleasant talks about work you've done there, the building types you encounter, parking and access quirks, nearby landmarks customers recognize. That specificity is what lets one small business outrank a bigger competitor for that pocket of the map. Build these pages only for areas you genuinely serve, one at a time, each with content you couldn't copy-paste to the next.

## Where the money gets wasted

Two purchases we see over and over:

- **Bulk directory submissions.** "500 citations for $99" mostly means low-quality directories no human visits — and a cleanup bill later when the duplicated, inconsistent listings need fixing. Citations matter; volume doesn't.
- **Generic high-volume keywords.** Ranking a small business for "SEO" or "lawyer" is a multi-year, big-budget fight for clicks that mostly aren't local anyway. The neighbourhood query you can actually win this quarter is worth more than the vanity keyword you can't.
