Most Canadian marketers file WeChat under "Chinese Facebook" and plan accordingly: set up a page, post content, buy reach. Then nothing happens, and WeChat gets written off as impenetrable.

The problem is the mental model. WeChat is not a social network with a feed you can buy your way into. It is a super-app — chat, group chats, Moments, Official Accounts, mini-programs, and payments in one place — and for a huge share of Chinese-speaking customers in Metro Vancouver, it is where daily life happens. We covered who those customers are in our [guide to marketing to Vancouver's Chinese community](/blog/marketing-to-vancouver-chinese-community/); this article is about how the WeChat piece actually works.

## Stop thinking "channel," start thinking "ecosystem"

On WeChat, there is no public timeline where a stranger discovers your brand. Everything moves through relationships: one-to-one chats, group chats, and a Moments feed visible only to contacts. That single fact should reorder your entire plan. Here is the priority order we recommend for a local Canadian business.

### 1. Personal and service accounts, plus group chats

This is where local commerce actually lives. Realtors, mortgage brokers, tutors, immigration consultants, clinics, and restaurants running food-order groups — the transactions happen in chat, not on a page. A staffed account that answers questions quickly, in natural Chinese, is your storefront. Group chats you run yourself (a customer group, an order group, a neighbourhood group) are your retention engine.

### 2. Being recommendable in community groups

Metro Vancouver runs on WeChat groups: neighbourhood groups, parent groups, deal-sharing groups, hobby groups. You mostly cannot advertise into them — but you can be *recommended* into them. When someone asks a group "anyone know a good dentist in Burnaby?", the answer that gets posted is the whole game. That means being present, genuinely helpful, easy to name-drop, and never spammy. Group etiquette is real, and violating it gets you removed and remembered.

### 3. Official Accounts — useful, but not first

An Official Account is WeChat's version of owned media. **Subscription accounts** can publish daily but live folded inside a subscriptions tab; **service accounts** publish four times a month but appear directly in chat lists and unlock payment and API features. Either way, know this going in: follower growth is hard. There is no discovery feed. Followers come from QR codes at your physical location, links forwarded through groups, and Moments shares. An Official Account amplifies trust you have already built at levels one and two — it cannot substitute for them.

### 4. Moments as the trust layer

For service providers especially, Moments functions like a personal newsletter to warm contacts. Consistent, human posts — projects completed, clients helped (with permission), festival greetings, glimpses of the person behind the business — compound into the credibility that makes referrals feel safe.

## Content norms worth respecting

WeChat has its own content culture. Long-form articles still perform — this is one of the few ecosystems where a 1,500-word piece gets read and forwarded. Poster-style images with text designed in (event details, menus, promotions) are the native visual format, because they survive forwarding into groups. And red packets at Lunar New Year and other festivals are a genuine engagement ritual, not a gimmick — used well, they are one of the few "promotions" the culture actively welcomes.

## The mistakes Western brands keep making

Three patterns account for most failures we see. Treating WeChat as a broadcast channel — pushing announcements at an audience that only responds to conversation. Machine-translated content — instantly recognizable, and it signals that the brand does not actually employ anyone who speaks to this community. And ignoring group etiquette — dropping promo links into groups uninvited, which burns the exact word-of-mouth network you came for.
