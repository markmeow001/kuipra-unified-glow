For years, keyword research meant one thing: find terms with high search volume and tolerable competition, then write pages to match. That playbook is breaking — not because search is dying, but because a growing share of answers now come from AI Overviews and assistants instead of blue links.

Here's the uncomfortable part: for small businesses, the volume-chasing playbook was already a losing game. Now it's worse. The way out is the same in both cases — stop optimizing for volume, start optimizing for intent.

## Why chasing volume was always a trap — and is now a bigger one

A head term like "social media marketing" has enormous volume and near-zero attainability for a small agency. The first page belongs to global publishers and software companies with years of accumulated authority. That was true before AI.

What's new: generic informational queries are exactly the ones AI Overviews and chat assistants answer directly. When someone asks "what is social media marketing?", the answer appears above the results — synthesized, cited, done. Even if you somehow ranked, the click increasingly doesn't happen.

The independent data points the same way. Pew Research Center tracked the browsing of 900 US adults in March 2025: on Google visits that showed an AI summary, people clicked a regular result 8% of the time, against 15% when there was no summary, and clicked a link inside the summary itself on just 1% of visits ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/)). In a vendor study built on aggregated Search Console data, SEO tool maker Ahrefs found that by December 2025 the presence of an AI Overview correlated with a 58% lower click-through rate for the top-ranking page ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overviews-reduce-clicks-update/)).

So high-volume generic keywords now combine the worst of both worlds: you probably can't rank, and if you did, the traffic is evaporating. The queries that still send visitors — and customers — are the specific ones.

## From keywords to questions

People don't type fragments into assistants; they ask full questions. "Best way to promote a community event in Vancouver on a small budget" is a query now, not a conversation. Your research should mirror that shift.

The richest question sources aren't in any SEO tool. They're in your own business:

- **Sales emails and DMs** — the questions prospects ask before they buy are the questions others are asking a search engine or an assistant right now.
- **Customer conversations** — objections, confusions, "how does X work with Y" moments.
- **Reviews** — yours and your competitors'. Review language is buyer language, verbatim.

Keep a running list of these questions. It will outperform any keyword tool export, because every entry comes with proof that a real buyer asked it.

One honest caveat: question-shaped searches are also the ones most likely to get an AI answer. In Pew's data, 60% of searches that began with "who", "what", "when" or "why" produced an AI summary, compared with 8% of one- or two-word searches. So treat your question list as a map of what buyers need to know, and write answers you'd be happy to see quoted. The clicks come from the questions closest to a decision.

## The three intent tiers

Every query sits on a ladder of intent:

### Informational — "what is…", "how does…"

The searcher wants understanding, not a vendor. This tier has the most volume and the least commercial value — and it's the tier AI answers most completely. Publish here selectively, to demonstrate expertise, not to harvest traffic.

### Comparative — "X vs Y", "best… for…"

The searcher is weighing options. "Agency vs in-house social media", "best marketing approach for a restaurant opening". Assistants answering these queries name specific options — and searchers still click through to validate. In a July 2026 BrightLocal survey of US consumers, 75% of people who used AI to find a local business checked Google or Maps before contacting it ([eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/shoppers-use-ai-find-local-businesses--then-double-check-on-google)). This is where small businesses start winning.

### Transactional — "pricing", "near me", "hire"

The searcher is ready to act. Volume is tiny; value is maximal. A page that cleanly answers "how much does social media management cost in Vancouver" will never trend — it will just quietly produce enquiries.

For local businesses, this is also the tier AI has changed least. In Whitespark's 2025 study of 540 queries across three US cities, AI Overviews appeared on 15% of local-intent searches, while the local map pack appeared on 93% ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/blog/case-study-the-prevalence-of-ai-overviews-in-local-search/)). "Near me" and neighbourhood searches still run through your Google Business Profile and the basics in our [local SEO guide](/blog/local-seo-guide-vancouver-small-business/). And once those searchers land, the page has to turn them into enquiries, which is what our [conversion audit](/blog/website-conversion-audit-checklist/) checks.

The strategy writes itself: concede the informational head terms, own the comparative and transactional long tail. There, being genuinely local and genuinely specific is an advantage no large publisher can copy.

## One page, one intent — but a whole question cluster

Long-tail doesn't mean one page per micro-variation. Search engines and AI systems both understand that "how much does an agency cost", "agency pricing", and "what should I budget for social media" are one intent expressed three ways.

Build one well-structured page per **query intent**: a direct answer up top, `##` subheadings for the natural follow-up questions, and an FAQ section with self-contained answers. That single page can rank — and be cited — across the entire cluster. What kills a page is the opposite mistake: mixing intents, so a pricing page drifts into a general explainer and serves no one fully.

This matters more with AI search in the mix. Google says AI Overviews and AI Mode may use a "query fan-out" technique, running several related searches across subtopics to build one answer ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). Our reading: a page that already answers the natural follow-up questions gives those searches more to find.

## Free research beats expensive tools

You can do all of the above without a subscription:

1. **Autocomplete** — type your service plus "for", "vs", "in", and read what suggests. That's real query data.
2. **People Also Ask** — expand a few entries and the box refreshes with more; it maps question clusters for you.
3. **Google Search Console** — the queries you *almost* rank for (positions 8–20) are your cheapest wins. Traffic from AI Overviews and AI Mode is counted in the same Performance report, under the "Web" search type. Since August 31, 2026, every site also gets a Generative AI performance report showing which pages appear in those features, but it reports impressions only, by page, country, device and date, with no query list ([Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16984139)). The keyword work still happens in the main report.
4. **Review language** — the exact phrases customers use are the phrases future customers search with.

What about Google Keyword Planner? Google calls it a free tool ([Google](https://business.google.com/us/ad-tools/keyword-planner/)), but you have to finish setting up a Google Ads account, billing details included, before it will suggest keywords ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7337243)). It's useful for seasonality and the rough size of broad demand. It's weakest exactly where small businesses win: Google notes that keywords with very low search volumes aren't discoverable in it, and that is where a lot of local, question-shaped demand lives. If you do run a small ads test, its search terms report shows the searches that actually triggered your ads, which is the sequence we lay out in [Google Ads vs. SEO](/blog/google-ads-vs-seo-small-business/).
