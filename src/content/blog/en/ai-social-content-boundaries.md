Every week a brand somewhere gets caught: an AI "customer" in the reviews, a burger on the menu board that the kitchen can't produce, a testimonial from a person who doesn't exist. The tools made these things easy. They didn't make them okay.

At Kuipra we use AI across our social content production, and we say so. What keeps that workable isn't restraint by vibes — it's a written boundary. Here's ours, in three zones, ready to steal.

## The green zone: automate freely

These tasks are AI's home turf. No disclosure debate, no ethical gray — just leverage:

- **Background and concept imagery.** Abstract visuals, mood boards, seasonal backdrops, illustration-style graphics. Nobody assumes a gradient or a stylized skyline is a photograph of anything.
- **Resizing and adaptation.** Turning one hero asset into nine placements — Stories, Reels covers, LinkedIn, banner crops. Pure production work; automate all of it.
- **Draft captions.** First-pass copy that a human rewrites for voice. The draft is AI's; the publish button is yours. (We've written up [exactly how that editing workflow runs](/blog/ai-content-workflow-without-sounding-like-ai/).)
- **Translation first drafts.** A starting point for bilingual content that a native speaker then makes actually native.
- **Brainstorming.** Angles, hooks, ideas for your [content calendar](/blog/content-calendar-for-small-business/), objection lists. Generating options is free; choosing is still your job. (The same approach works for [competitor research](/blog/ai-competitor-research-workflow/), as long as you verify what you act on.)

The common thread: in the green zone, AI output is either obviously synthetic or fully human-reviewed before anyone sees it. No viewer is being asked to believe something false.

## The yellow zone: use with disclosure and care

Some uses are legitimate but sit close enough to the line that they need rules attached.

### AI imagery involving your product

An AI-assisted render of your product in a lifestyle scene can work — if the product shown is truthful to the product sold. Same size, same color, same features, no invented details. The moment the image looks better than the thing a customer receives, you've made a promise you can't keep, and every unboxing becomes a small betrayal.

Canada's advertising self-regulator takes the same line. Ad Standards' [Influencer Marketing Disclosure Guidelines](https://adstandards.ca/wp-content/uploads/Ad-Standards-Influencer-Marketing-Disclosure-Guidelines-2025-10-08.pdf) (Fall 2025 update) say that when an image showing a product's benefits or attributes is generated by AI or significantly altered with it, that should be disclosed, with tags like #MadeWithAI or #AIcreated. They also say such images "must remain truthful as to the benefits, results and attributes of the product or service," and then add the sentence every marketer should remember: "A disclaimer cannot correct a misleading impression." The guidelines are industry best practice, not law, but Ad Standards' council may consider them when it rules on complaints under the Canadian Code of Advertising Standards.

### AI voiceovers

Synthetic narration on Reels and explainers is now common and fine — with two conditions. Don't clone a real person's voice without written permission, and don't build a persona that implies a real spokesperson exists when none does. When in doubt, a simple "AI voice" note in the caption costs nothing.

### AI influencers and virtual spokespeople

Some brands now run a fully synthetic "creator". The same Ad Standards guidelines say an AI-generated influencer still has to disclose a material connection to the brand like any human creator, that best practice is to say the spokesperson is virtual (#VirtualInfluencer, #AIinfluencer or #AIcreated), and that it shouldn't give testimonials about things it cannot experience; their example is "this food sure tastes great!" Our [Vancouver influencer marketing guide](/blog/vancouver-influencer-marketing-guide/) covers the rest of the disclosure rules for paid creators.

Yellow-zone rule of thumb: ask what the viewer will *reasonably assume*. If they'd assume it's real and it isn't, fix the content first. A label can correct an assumption about *how* something was made; it can't correct a false impression of *what the customer gets*.

## The red zone: never, under any account login

These aren't judgment calls. They're bans:

- **Fake customer photos or testimonials.** An invented happy customer is a fabricated endorsement, full stop.
- **AI-generated "user" reviews.** Fake reviews are the textbook case of what consumer-protection regulators exist to punish. Canada's Competition Bureau has warned that anyone who writes or permits reviews giving a false or misleading impression could be liable ([Competition Bureau](https://www.canada.ca/en/competition-bureau/news/2024/01/online-reviews-posted-by-employees-businesses-could-be-liable.html)), and Google can block new reviews on a profile for a period and show customers a warning that fake reviews were removed ([Google](https://support.google.com/business/answer/14114287)). Our [Google reviews guide](/blog/google-reviews-management-guide/) covers how to earn real ones.
- **Fabricated before/after results.** Generating the "after" is manufacturing evidence for a claim your product didn't earn. Real before-and-afters are some of the strongest content a service business has ([our salon guide](/blog/beauty-salon-marketing-vancouver/) builds a whole strategy on them), which is exactly why faked ones do so much damage.
- **Deepfake-style content of real people.** Real faces, real voices, fake actions. Legal exposure aside, this is the fastest reputation-ender on this list.
- **AI food or product photography passed off as the real item.** If the dish in the photo can't come out of your kitchen, the photo is a lie with a price tag under it. A phone and good window light get you most of the way to an honest version ([our food photo guide](/blog/restaurant-food-visual-content-guide/)).

Notice what unites these: each one asks the audience to believe a false fact about the real world. That's not content creation. That's misrepresentation — and depending on your jurisdiction, it can breach consumer-protection rules long before it breaches anyone's patience.

In Canada, the Competition Bureau has started spelling this out. Its section of a September 2025 [Canadian Digital Regulators Forum paper on synthetic media](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/how-we-foster-competition/education-and-outreach/publications/canadian-digital-regulators-forum-synthetic-media-digital-landscape) notes that the Competition Act's rules against materially false or misleading representations cover illustrations and audio-visual promotions in any medium, and gives the example that "Deepfakes can make fake reviews and endorsements seem authentic." The Bureau says its deepfake examples are illustrative and none has yet been found to violate the Act by a court or tribunal, but it's clear which way it is looking.

## The platforms are forcing the issue anyway

The major platforms have been moving in one direction: labeling. AI-generated and AI-altered media increasingly gets flagged — by creator self-disclosure requirements, by automatic detection, or both, especially for photorealistic content. By late 2026 the rules are specific:

- **Meta (Facebook, Instagram, Threads)** requires people to use its AI disclosure tool when they post "a photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered," says it may apply penalties when they don't, and shows an "AI info" label when it detects industry signals such as C2PA metadata ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/02/meta-prepares-for-2026-us-midterms/)).
- **TikTok**'s Community Guidelines, in effect since September 24, 2026, "require creators to label AI-generated or significantly edited content that shows realistic-looking scenes or people"; unlabeled content "may be removed, restricted, or labeled" ([TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)).
- **YouTube** requires disclosure of realistic AI-generated or altered content at upload, can label videos itself when it detects AI or C2PA metadata, and may penalize creators who consistently don't disclose. Beauty filters, colour correction and AI help with a script or thumbnail don't need disclosure, and YouTube says disclosing alone won't limit a video's audience ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)).
- **Xiaohongshu and WeChat** operate under China's rules on labelling AI-generated content, in force since September 1, 2025, which require platforms to label it and users to declare it with the platform's labelling tool when they post ([Cyberspace Administration of China](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2025-03/14/c_1743654684782215.htm)).

Which means "quietly synthetic" is a strategy with a shrinking shelf life. If a platform label is going to appear on your content eventually, the only question is whether you disclosed first or got caught second. Those two look very different to an audience.

## The trust math

Here's the asymmetry every team should internalize: trust accrues in drips and drains in buckets. A hundred honest posts each earn you a little credibility. One exposed fake doesn't just cost its own value — it reprices everything you've ever published, because the audience's new question is "what else wasn't real?" There is no engagement rate that makes that trade good.

## A policy you can adopt this week

You don't need a legal department. You need one page:

1. **Green zone** — pre-approved, no sign-off needed: concept imagery, resizing, caption drafts, translation drafts, brainstorming.
2. **Yellow zone** — allowed with conditions: product imagery must match the real product and gets a #MadeWithAI-style tag when AI made or heavily altered it; AI voices need permission and, when ambiguous, a label; virtual spokespeople are disclosed as virtual and never vouch for what they can't experience.
3. **Red zone** — banned without exception: fake customers, fake reviews, fabricated results, deepfakes of real people, synthetic product photos presented as real.
4. **One owner.** A named person decides edge cases. Ambiguity defaults to yellow, and yellow defaults to disclosure.

Write it down, share it with everyone who touches the accounts, and revisit it quarterly — the tools will keep moving; the line shouldn't drift with them.
