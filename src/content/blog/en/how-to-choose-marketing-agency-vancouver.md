Let's get the disclosure out of the way: we're a marketing agency, so we have an obvious interest in how you make this decision. But we've also sat across the table from enough Vancouver business owners burned by their last agency to know that a clear-eyed buyer is a better client — for us and for whoever you end up choosing. So here's the guide we wish every prospect had read first. (Updated November 2026 with a section on judging the AI and GEO claims that now appear in almost every pitch.)

## Before you shop: know what you're actually buying

The most common purchasing mistake isn't picking the wrong agency. It's buying the wrong thing.

Most agency services fall into two buckets: **strategy** (positioning, audience, message, plan) and **execution** (content, ads, community management, production). Plenty of businesses buy execution — more posts, more ads — when the real problem is that nobody has decided what the brand should say or to whom. Execution on top of a vague strategy just makes the vagueness louder. We've written before about [why that order matters](/blog/brand-strategy-vs-marketing-tactics/); read that first if you're not sure which bucket your problem lives in.

If you can't articulate your positioning in one sentence, you need strategy before you need a content calendar — and the right first engagement is small and strategic (the kind of [brand strategy work](/services/brand-strategy/) that ends in a decision, not a deck), not a twelve-month execution retainer.

It also helps to know roughly what the work should cost before anyone quotes you. Our [channel-by-channel cost guide](/blog/vancouver-marketing-channels-costs/) gives planning ranges, and if you're torn between paying for ads now and building search visibility, settle [the Google Ads vs. SEO question](/blog/google-ads-vs-seo-small-business/) first. It changes which kind of agency you need.

## The six questions to ask any agency

1. **Who exactly works on my account?** The people in the pitch meeting are rarely the people doing the work. Ask for names, roles, and how many other accounts each person carries.
2. **Show me your thinking, not just your portfolio.** Finished work tells you what a client approved. Ask them to walk you through *why* a campaign was built the way it was — the reasoning is what you're actually hiring.
3. **How do you measure success, and what's in the monthly report?** Ask to see a real (anonymized) client report. If it's a wall of impressions and likes with no line connecting activity to business outcomes, that's what you'll be getting too.
4. **What happens in the first 90 days?** A good agency can describe a concrete onboarding: discovery, audit, strategy, then execution. "We'll hit the ground running" is not a plan.
5. **What do you need from us?** Agencies that promise results with zero involvement from you are lying. The honest answer includes your time — approvals, subject-matter input, access.
6. **Who owns the accounts and assets?** Ad accounts, social logins, creative files, audience data — all of it should live in accounts *you* own, with the agency granted access. An agency that insists on owning your ad account or holding your logins is building leverage for the day you try to leave. This one is non-negotiable, and Google backs it: its [guide for advertisers working with third parties](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/9457109) says an agency should set up a separate Ads account for you, and that you're entitled at minimum to your ads' clicks, impressions and total cost.

## Red flags that should end the conversation

- **Guaranteed rankings or follower counts.** Nobody controls Google or the Instagram algorithm. Google's own [advice on hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) puts it plainly: "No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google." Guarantees mean either ignorance or bought followers.
- **Guaranteed AI citations or "the top spot in ChatGPT."** Nobody controls what an AI assistant says either, and its answers change from one run to the next (more on that below). The honest version of this pitch is "we'll strengthen the signals AI tools rely on and track how often you appear."
- **Offers to "boost" your reviews.** Paying or rewarding customers for reviews, or only asking the happy ones, breaks [Google's review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114), and the Competition Bureau has warned that businesses [can be liable for misleading reviews](https://www.canada.ca/en/competition-bureau/news/2024/01/online-reviews-posted-by-employees-businesses-could-be-liable.html). Our [reviews guide](/blog/google-reviews-management-guide/) covers the legitimate way to earn them.
- **A package quoted before they've asked about your business.** One-size pricing before discovery means one-size work after it.
- **No questions for you.** If the whole meeting is them presenting, they're selling a product, not solving your problem.
- **Long lock-in contracts with no exit.** Confident agencies earn renewal monthly. Twelve months with no out clause is a retention strategy substituting for a results strategy.
- **Vague reporting commitments.** "We'll keep you updated" is not a reporting cadence.

## How to judge AI and GEO claims

Almost every pitch now includes an AI line: "AI-powered content," "GEO," "we'll get you recommended by ChatGPT." Some of it is real work; some is ordinary SEO relabelled at a higher price. Three questions separate the two:

- **"What will you do differently for AI search?"** Google's [guidance on its AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) says optimizing for generative AI search is still SEO, and that no special files or markup are required. A credible answer sounds like the fundamentals in our [GEO guide](/blog/generative-engine-optimization-guide/): pages that answer real questions clearly, consistent business details everywhere, reviews and mentions you've earned, and AI search crawlers allowed in. If the main deliverable is an llms.txt file or a special "AI schema" package, ask why; Google says its Search ignores llms.txt.
- **"How will you measure it?"** AI answers aren't a fixed ranking. In a [January 2026 study by SparkToro and Gumshoe](https://sparktoro.com/blog/new-research-ais-are-highly-inconsistent-when-recommending-brands-or-products-marketers-should-take-care-when-tracking-ai-visibility/) covering 2,961 responses from ChatGPT, Claude and Google's AI, there was less than a 1-in-100 chance that ChatGPT or Google's AI would return the same list of brands twice. One screenshot of one prompt proves very little. Ask for the same prompts repeated over time, and for the numbers that matter more: calls, bookings, form fills. For local businesses, [how AI assistants decide who to recommend](/blog/how-ai-assistants-recommend-local-businesses/) explains what those tools lean on.
- **"Where does AI sit in your production, and who checks it?"** Using AI is fine; publishing unedited volume isn't. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) name generating many pages with AI "without adding value for users" as scaled content abuse. A good agency can show you its workflow and tell you what it never automates. We've published [ours](/blog/ai-content-workflow-without-sounding-like-ai/).

## The fit dimensions almost everyone ignores

**Size match.** A small business at a large agency gets the junior team and the leftover attention. You want to be a meaningful client, not the smallest logo on their slide.

**Language and cultural capability.** In Metro Vancouver this matters more than almost anywhere in North America. If the Chinese-Canadian market is part of your growth plan, "we can translate posts" is not the same as native-level content and platform fluency. Ask who writes it, not who translates it.

**Sector conflicts.** Ask whether they serve your direct competitors, and how they handle it. There's no universal right answer, but an agency that hasn't thought about it is a problem.

## Understand what the pricing model buys you

- **Retainers** buy continuity and priority — but a suspiciously cheap retainer almost always means junior hands and template work. The math has to fund the seniority you were promised.
- **Project pricing** fits defined deliverables with clear ends: a rebrand, a launch, a strategy sprint.
- **Performance pricing** sounds aligned but often pushes agencies toward short-term levers that juice the metric while quietly eroding the brand.

None of these is inherently right. What matters is that the agency can explain the tradeoff honestly instead of just defending the model they sell. One newer question worth asking: if AI tools make their production faster, how is that reflected in what you pay and in how much senior time you get?

## Reduce the risk: start with a trial project

You don't have to decide everything on day one. A scoped four-to-eight-week engagement — a strategy sprint, one campaign, a single-channel audit — shows you how an agency thinks, communicates, and reports, at a fraction of a retainer's commitment. Agencies confident in their work welcome this. The ones that push hard for the annual contract before proving anything are answering your most important question for you.
