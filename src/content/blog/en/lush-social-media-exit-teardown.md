Most brands treat social media as oxygen. In November 2021, Lush turned it off — and lived.

The usual disclosure first: **Lush is not a Kuipra client.** This is an outside teardown of a public brand, based on its stores, its own statements, and widely reported coverage. We pick brands with a local thread when we can, and Lush qualifies: its first North American shop opened on Vancouver's Denman Street in 1996 ([BCBusiness](https://bcbusiness.ca/business/retail/meet-the-woman-who-made-vancouvers-lush-a-cosmetics-powerhouse/)), and the city was long one of its manufacturing bases, until Lush said in 2024 it would shift that production to Toronto ([Daily Hive](https://dailyhive.com/vancouver/lush-vancouver-manufacturing-close-layoffs)).

Here's the decision worth studying. At the height of the Facebook Papers revelations about platform harm to teenagers, Lush switched off its Instagram, Facebook, TikTok and Snapchat accounts in all 48 countries where it trades ([Lush's announcement](https://weare.lush.com/press-releases/lush-is-taking-fresh-approach-social/)). Not a weekend stunt, not a single market — the global accounts went dark, and co-founder Mark Constantine said he was "happy to lose £10m" in sales over it ([Cosmetics Business](https://cosmeticsbusiness.com/lush-boss-mark-constantine-happy-to-lose-10m-after-quitting-social-media--180904)). A cosmetics brand whose product photographs like candy walked away from the world's biggest photo platforms.

So what does a brand do with no feed to fill?

## The exit was the campaign

Start with the uncomfortable irony: quitting social media generated more coverage than any Lush campaign ever ran on it. Global press, opinion columns, case studies (this one included) — years of earned media from a single decision, because the decision was *costly*.

That's the mechanic to understand. Anyone can claim values in a caption; a claim that costs millions is legible in a way no post can be. A bath brand saying "we care about teen wellbeing" is wallpaper. A bath brand walking away from its TikTok over it is news. Values become brand assets exactly at the point where you pay something for them — which is why most "purpose marketing" produces nothing: it risks nothing.

## Owned channels were already load-bearing

The exit only worked because Lush had spent years building channels no platform could repossess:

- **An email list and app** that carry launches, restocks and storytelling directly to people who asked for them.
- **A website that behaves like a magazine** — ingredient stories, campaign explainers, product education that search engines can find.
- **Stores built as demonstrations.** Product you're invited to touch, sinks for testing, staff trained to perform the product. The shop is the content studio.

Note the order of operations: own the relationship first, *then* renegotiate with the platforms. A business that quits Instagram with no email list hasn't made a values statement; it has simply vanished. We've written about why [email is the highest-leverage channel most local businesses neglect](/blog/email-marketing-for-local-businesses/) — Lush is what that argument looks like at full scale. If many of your customers live on WeChat, the owned equivalent is a contact list on an account you control, which our [WeChat private traffic playbook](/blog/wechat-private-traffic-playbook/) walks through. And because the voice now has to carry across email, app, site and stores with no feed to lean on, it helps to have it written down; our [brand voice guide](/blog/building-a-brand-voice-guide/) shows how.

## The content kept flowing — without the brand

Search any platform Lush left and you'll still find it everywhere: bath-bomb dissolves, shower-jelly demos, haul videos, ingredient debates. The brand's accounts went dark; the brand's *presence* didn't, because the product itself is engineered to be filmed. Fizzing colour, glitter, named characters like the Sex Bomb and Intergalactic — every purchase is a potential thirty-second video with built-in payoff. Like the critters in our [TELUS teardown](/blog/telus-critters-brand-teardown/), the product is recognisable before anyone sees a logo, so other people's videos still read as Lush.

We saw the same design logic in our [Aritzia teardown](/blog/aritzia-social-strategy-teardown/): make the product and the space do the performing, and customers become the media. Lush pushed the idea to its logical extreme — the customers kept broadcasting after the brand unplugged its own transmitter.

## Five years on: still off, and choosier

The obvious question five years later is whether the exit stuck. It has. As of late 2026, Lush has not posted or advertised on Instagram, Facebook or TikTok since 2021, it stopped posting on X in 2023 after Elon Musk's takeover, and its TikTok holds a single post that reads "Be somewhere else" ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/marketing/how-lush-quit-social-media-ads-and-still-drove-growth/)). The one new social platform is Bluesky, which Lush joined in June 2026 after what a spokesperson called a "detailed review." The same spokesperson said the company remains "firm in our absence from Meta platforms, TikTok, Snapchat, and X" ([BeautyMatter](https://beautymatter.com/articles/lushs-social-media-comeback-has-conditions)). It has also started a Substack, and it keeps organic, unpaid YouTube and Pinterest channels. Julia Hamfelt, head of marketing for Lush North America, told Digiday: "There are some hard lines in the sand."

The financials since 2021 aren't a clean test of the exit, because cocoa prices and a consolidation of manufacturing in Europe and Canada also moved the numbers. But the latest results show growth without those feeds: turnover was £736.2m for the year to June 2025, with like-for-like turnover up 8% (stores up 7%, digital platforms up 13%) ([Cosmetics Business](https://www.cosmeticsbusiness.com/lush-reports-full-year-2025-results)).

The update sharpens the lesson. Lush never quit "social media" as a category. It quit specific platforms on stated conditions, and it joins new ones only when they meet those conditions. That's a stated channel policy, not a mood. Fluevog made a similar call about Amazon in 2018, because its brand "didn't feel special" there ([Fluevog teardown](/blog/fluevog-community-teardown/)). Compare [Duolingo](/blog/duolingo-social-media-teardown/), which held back its edgy posts for several months in 2025 and found its growth leaned on the very feed it had toned down. Silence is only a strategy when the relationship already lives somewhere else.

## What this does and doesn't license

Be careful with the moral of this story. Lush could afford the exit because it entered with three assets most businesses don't have: decades of name recognition, guaranteed press interest, and high-street foot traffic that discovers the brand by walking past it. A two-year-old café that deletes its Instagram doesn't get thinkpieces; it gets forgotten.

The transferable lesson is about *dependency*, not absence:

1. **Audit where your customer relationships actually live.** If the honest answer is "Meta's servers," you're a policy change away from starting over.
2. **Convert rented reach into owned reach, continuously.** Every follower you can move to an email list, a booking system, or a loyalty program is a customer an algorithm can't hide from you.
3. **Let values cost something before you advertise them.** A stand that risks nothing reads as decoration, because it is.
4. **Make the product filmable and the platforms matter less.** If customers generate your content, your account is optional; if your account generates your content, you're on a treadmill you can never leave. ## The takeaway

Lush's exit isn't an argument that social media is worthless — it's proof of what becomes possible when a brand refuses to let rented channels hold the whole relationship. Build the list, design the experience worth filming, and make your values expensive enough to believe. Then social media becomes a choice, not a life-support system.
