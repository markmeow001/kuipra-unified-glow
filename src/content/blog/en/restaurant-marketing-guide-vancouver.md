Vancouver has one of the most competitive restaurant markets in North America — and most of its restaurants market themselves in exactly the wrong order: ads before the basics, followers before regulars, everything before the list.

Here's the playbook we'd hand any Vancouver restaurant in 2026, in the order that compounds.

## Layer 1: Win the map before anything else

Before a new diner sees your Instagram, they see your Google Business Profile — the photos, the rating, the review count, how recently people reviewed, whether the hours look trustworthy. This single free asset outweighs every other channel, and most restaurants leave it half-finished.

The complete foundation: every field filled, current menu (as text and photos, not just a PDF), 30+ real photos with dishes shot in good light, [holiday hours](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6303076) updated, and — the part that separates winners — **a review system, not review luck**: QR code at the table or on the receipt, staff who ask when a guest compliments the food, and a reply to every single review. Our [Google Business Profile guide](/blog/google-business-profile-guide-vancouver/) and [review management guide](/blog/google-reviews-management-guide/) cover the mechanics step by step.

Keep the asking clean. Google's [review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114), updated in February 2026, bans offering anything for a review (the "free dessert for a review" card is out), bans asking only happy guests, and now says businesses shouldn't require or pressure guests to review while they're on the premises, or ask them to include specific content. In our reading, a QR card on the table is fine; a server waiting beside the table until the review is posted is not. Google's penalties for policy-breaking reviews include [blocking new reviews for a period and showing diners a warning that fake reviews were removed](https://support.google.com/business/answer/14114287), which costs far more than the reviews were worth.

Once Google is right, make sure Apple Maps, Bing and the directories say the same thing. A wrong phone number from an old listing keeps answering for you; our [citations guide](/blog/local-business-citations-guide/) lists what to fix first.

One finding to internalize: a [Harvard Business School working paper](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:hbs:wpaper:12-016) by Michael Luca found that a one-star increase in Yelp rating led to a 5–9% increase in revenue for independent restaurants in Washington State, while chain restaurants weren't affected. It's older Yelp data, so treat the exact size as directional. The direction is the point: reviews aren't a vanity metric in food — they're the product page.

## Layer 2: Make the food do the marketing

Restaurants have the single greatest content advantage of any business: the product photographs itself, customers *want* to film it, and every table is a potential production set. The strategy is to engineer that, not hope for it:

- **One signature, named and filmable.** A dish with a name, a moment (the pour, the flame, the pull-apart) and a reason to point a phone at it. This is the restaurant version of the named-product play we broke down in our [Aritzia teardown](/blog/aritzia-social-strategy-teardown/).
- **Short video over static posts.** Reels and TikTok reach non-followers; a phone, good light and 20 seconds of the kitchen doing something real beats a studio shoot. Our [short-form video playbook](/blog/short-form-video-guide-vancouver/) has formats a small team can keep up every week.
- **Let customers be the camera crew.** Repost their content (with credit), make the interior and plating repost-worthy, and put the handle where phones already are.

For the shot list, phone technique and what's worth paying a photographer for, see our [food photo and video guide](/blog/restaurant-food-visual-content-guide/). Running a café or bubble tea shop instead? The check-in economy works a little differently there, and our [café marketing guide](/blog/cafe-marketing-guide-vancouver/) covers it.

## Layer 3: The Chinese-platform advantage

If your food appeals to Chinese-speaking diners at all, Xiaohongshu (RedNote) is Vancouver's most underpriced restaurant channel — searchable, recommendation-driven, and, in our experience, where many Chinese-speaking diners in the city decide where to eat. It behaves differently from Instagram (search and saves beat follows; authentic notes beat ads), and we wrote a [dedicated Xiaohongshu playbook for Vancouver restaurants](/blog/xiaohongshu-restaurant-marketing-vancouver/) covering exactly how to work it — plus a [broader Xiaohongshu guide](/blog/xiaohongshu-marketing-vancouver/) if you're deciding between doing it in-house and hiring an agency.

The broader principle from our [Chinese community marketing guide](/blog/marketing-to-vancouver-chinese-community/) applies here too: a translated caption is not a localized presence. The restaurants that win this audience run the platform natively — right format, right occasions, right dishes forward.

## Layer 4: Delivery apps — use them, don't marry them

The economics are brutal. On their Canadian pricing pages, [DoorDash](https://merchants.doordash.com/en-ca/pricing) lists delivery commissions of 20%, 25% or 29% depending on the plan, and [Uber Eats](https://merchants.ubereats.com/ca/en/pricing/) lists 20%, 25% or 30%; pickup orders carry lower rates. That can swallow most of a restaurant's margin. But the apps also hold real discovery traffic — new customers you'd never otherwise reach.

BC adds a rule worth knowing before you pick a plan. The province's [Food Delivery Service Fee Act](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/employment-business/business/managing-a-business/starting-a-business/starting-a-restaurant-in-bc/food-delivery-service-fee) caps fees for core ordering and delivery at 20% of the order, excluding tax and tip, for platforms that serve 500 or more restaurants. Anything above that has to be an enhanced service you agreed to, such as extra promotion or lower customer fees, and you must be able to cancel back to the capped tier. If you're paying 25–30%, check every quarter whether the extra visibility is bringing orders you wouldn't get on the base plan.

The sane strategy is asymmetric: **acquire on the apps, retain off them.** Keep a presence for discovery; then move repeat business direct — a card in the bag with a direct-order discount, pickup incentives, a phone number that answers. Every regular you shift from a 20–30% channel to a direct one is margin recovered.

## Layer 5: Own your regulars

Restaurants obsess over new customers while their revenue actually lives in the second, fifth and twentieth visit. The tools are boring and extremely effective:

- **A list.** Email or SMS, collected at reservation, WiFi login, or with a receipt QR — then actually used: new menu items, holiday pre-orders, slow-night offers. [Email remains the highest-ROI channel a local business can own.](/blog/email-marketing-for-local-businesses/) Under [CASL](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/page-2.html), a purchase gives you implied consent to message someone for two years, so ask for explicit consent at sign-up if you want to keep them longer. For Chinese-speaking regulars, the list often lives on WeChat; our [WeChat private traffic playbook](/blog/wechat-private-traffic-playbook/) covers how to run it.
- **A reason to return.** Loyalty stamps, a birthday dish, early access to seasonal menus — simple beats clever.
- **The calendar.** Vancouver dining runs on moments — Dine Out, Lunar New Year, patio season, holiday parties. Plan them in advance with our [seasonal marketing calendar](/blog/vancouver-seasonal-marketing-calendar/) instead of reacting the week before.

## What we'd skip

Broad brand-awareness ads before the map is won. Discount platforms that train bargain hunters who never return at full price. And chasing every platform at once — a restaurant with a killer GBP, one strong short-video channel and a working list beats one with six half-dead accounts.
