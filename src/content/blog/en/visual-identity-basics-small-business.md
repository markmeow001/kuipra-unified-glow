Ask a small-business owner about their visual identity and the conversation goes straight to the logo — how much it cost, whether it's time for a new one. But your logo is probably the least-examined thing you publish. What people actually see, hundreds of times a year, is a post at thumbnail size moving past their thumb at scroll speed.

So here's the test that matters: if we covered your name, would a follower still know a post was yours? Visual identity is a recognition system. The goal is to be recognized *before* the name is read. Everything below is in service of that — and none of it requires a big budget. It requires decisions, made once, kept ruthlessly.

## The minimum viable identity kit

Four decisions. That's the whole kit.

### One primary colour, one accent — used everywhere

Not a palette of six. One colour that owns your brand, one accent for contrast, applied to every post, sign, and document without exception. Restraint is what creates recognition, not spend: a single colour repeated two hundred times beats six colours rotated tastefully.

### Two typefaces, with clear jobs

One for headlines, one for everything else — and write the roles down. A third font isn't an option; it's a decision someone will make differently every time, and each improvisation chips away at the pattern people were starting to learn.

### A photo style, not a camera

Lighting and tone matter far more than gear. Phone photos shot in the same kind of light and edited the same way every time will build recognition; a beautiful professional shoot mixed into a feed of random snaps won't. Pick your light (bright and airy, or warm and moody), pick one editing treatment, and stop negotiating with yourself per post.

### Logo variations for the places it actually lives

Your logo lives in a profile circle, a page header, a favicon, and maybe a storefront sign — four very different sizes and shapes. A detailed logo dies at 40 pixels. You need a simplified mark for small placements, and you need the files exported *before* the day someone asks for them.

## Where consistency pays first

If you can only enforce the system in a few places, enforce it here:

- **The social feed grid.** Nine recent posts, seen in one glance, is your storefront window. This is where the covered-name test is passed or failed.
- **Packaging and signage.** Physical encounters repeat with the same customers — repetition is the whole mechanism.
- **Proposals and invoices.** The identity should keep working while money changes hands, not dissolve into a default Word template at the exact moment trust matters.
- **Email.** Header, colours, sign-off. Low effort, high frequency.

## The mistakes that erase recognition

We see the same four constantly: a different template every post, because novelty feels like effort. Five fonts, because each new graphic invited a new choice. A logo too intricate to survive small sizes, so it gets inconsistently cropped instead. And the most expensive one — the trend-chasing rebrand. Aesthetic consistency compounds; it's the same lesson we pulled from [our Aritzia teardown](/blog/aritzia-social-strategy-teardown/), where a feed reads as one continuous visual system you'd recognize without the handle. A refresh every eighteen months resets that meter to zero and hands the compounding back.

## Put the system on one page

Colours with hex codes. Both typefaces and their roles. Three or four photo do/don't examples. Logo files listed by placement. One line on what never happens ("we never put text over faces"). That's the entire document — the same logic as [a one-page brand voice guide](/blog/building-a-brand-voice-guide/), applied to visuals. Now any freelancer, staff member, or Canva session produces on-brand work without you in the room.

## The bilingual layer

For many Vancouver businesses, the identity has to work in English and Chinese — and this is where DIY systems quietly break. Your Latin typeface has no Chinese companion by default; choose a pairing that matches its weight and mood, or the two languages will look like two brands. And Chinese is denser: the same message takes fewer characters carrying more visual weight each, so a layout sized for an English sentence can feel empty or cramped in translation. Test every template with both languages before you lock it.
