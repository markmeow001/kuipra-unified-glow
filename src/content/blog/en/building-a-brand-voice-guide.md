Most brand voice guides share the same fate: written once, admired in a kickoff meeting, then buried in a drive folder while everyone goes back to writing captions from instinct. Six months later the Instagram sounds like an intern, the email sounds like a lawyer, and the website sounds like nobody in particular.

The problem usually isn't effort. It's that the guide was written to be *read*, not *used*. Here's how we build ones that survive contact with a real content calendar.

## Voice is personality. Tone is the adjustment.

Get this distinction right and half the confusion disappears.

**Voice** is who your brand is — the personality that stays constant across every channel and every situation. **Tone** is how that personality flexes for the moment: upbeat in a product launch, warm and steady in a customer complaint, brisk in a shipping notification.

A person is a useful analogy. You're the same person at a wedding and at a funeral — but you don't behave identically at both. A voice guide that ignores tone produces brands that sound tone-deaf in hard moments. A guide that only lists tones, with no stable voice underneath, produces brands with no recognizable personality at all.

Voice is a strategy decision, which means it should follow from your positioning. If you haven't written that down yet, start with our guide to [writing a positioning statement](/blog/how-to-write-a-positioning-statement/) — voice expresses positioning; it can't replace it.

## Define voice on three axes

"Friendly and professional" describes every brand on earth, which means it describes none. Instead, place your brand on three spectrums and — this is the part most guides skip — write down *why*.

- **Formal ↔ Casual.** Where do you sit, and why? ("Casual, because our customers are exhausted by corporate wellness-speak.")
- **Playful ↔ Serious.** ("Mostly serious, because people trust us with their money — but never stiff.")
- **Bold ↔ Measured.** ("Bold, because our whole positioning is that the industry is too timid.")

Pick a position on each axis, not the midpoint. "Somewhere in the middle" is not a decision — it's a deferral, and every writer will resolve it differently. The written *why* is what makes the guide usable: when a new writer faces a sentence the examples don't cover, the reasoning tells them how to decide.

Three axes is deliberate. Five feels more thorough and gets remembered by no one.

## Do/don't pairs are the whole ballgame

If your team reads only one section, make it this one. Abstract adjectives don't transfer; examples do. Take a generic sentence and rewrite it in your voice:

> **Don't:** "We are pleased to announce the launch of our new seasonal menu."
> **Do:** "The fall menu is here. We've been arguing about it for months — come tell us who was right."

> **Don't:** "We apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused."
> **Do:** "That's on us. Here's what happened, and here's what we're doing about it."

(These are archetypes — yours should be rewritten in *your* voice, on your three axes.) Five to eight pairs covering your recurring situations — announcement, apology, promotion, education — will do more for consistency than ten pages of description. This is the section writers actually open on deadline.

## One voice, four channels

Run your voice through your real channels and note how tone shifts while voice holds:

- **Website** — voice at its most complete. Full sentences, room for the reasoning.
- **Instagram** — same personality, compressed. Shorter rhythm, more energy, but the same person talking.
- **Email** — more personal, more direct. Written to one reader, not an audience.
- **Customer complaints** — the real test. Playfulness steps back; warmth and directness step forward. A brand that cracks jokes in a refund dispute has confused voice with shtick.

If your voice only works in celebratory posts, it isn't a voice yet — it's a mood.

## Bilingual brands: define voice per language, don't translate it

This one matters for many of the Vancouver businesses we work with. A voice guide written in English and run through translation produces Chinese copy that is technically correct and personality-free.

The personality should stay consistent — but the *devices* that express it are language-specific. Playfulness in English might lean on wordplay and deadpan understatement; the same playfulness in Chinese may live in rhythm, in a well-chosen idiom, or in a knowing cultural reference that has no English equivalent. Write the axes once, then write native do/don't examples for each language, ideally by a writer who thinks in that language. Anything less and one audience always gets the diluted version of your brand.

## Keep it to one page, then test it

A voice guide is a tool, not [a strategy deck](/blog/brand-strategy-vs-marketing-tactics/) — and tools earn their keep by being used. One page: three axes with positions and reasons, your do/don't pairs, a line on tone by channel.

Then run the only test that matters: hand it to a writer who has never touched your brand and have them draft three posts. If the drafts sound like you, the guide works. If they don't, the gap between their copy and your expectations is a precise map of what the guide failed to say. Fix the guide, not the writer.
