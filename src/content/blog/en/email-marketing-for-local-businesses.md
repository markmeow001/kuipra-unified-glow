Every year, some social platform changes its algorithm and a wave of local businesses discovers their "audience" was rented, not owned. The account that reached thousands last spring reaches hundreds this fall, and there's nothing to appeal. Email is the opposite deal: nobody stands between you and the inbox. The list is yours, it moves with you between tools, and no algorithm decides who sees your message.

That's why we tell clients the same thing again and again: before you agonize over a fourth social platform, build the list.

## Why email beats another platform

Three reasons, in order of importance:

1. **You own it.** Export the list, switch providers, keep going. Try that with your Instagram followers.
2. **It's algorithm-proof.** Reach doesn't decay because a platform decided video is the new priority.
3. **It's the highest-intent audience you have.** Nobody accidentally joins an email list. Everyone on it made a small deliberate choice to hear from you — which is more than you can say for most followers.

Email doesn't replace social; it catches the people social introduced you to and keeps them.

## Building the list as a local business

You have something online-only brands would kill for: people physically standing in front of you. Use that.

- **The point-of-sale ask.** "Want early access to our seasonal specials? Takes ten seconds." Said by a human at checkout, this converts better than any popup.
- **A QR code at the counter.** Link it straight to a one-field signup form. Not your homepage — the form.
- **Booking-flow opt-in.** If customers book online, add an unchecked opt-in box to the flow. Unchecked matters: they tick it, you don't.
- **A lead magnet that fits your trade.** A landscaper's seasonal yard checklist, a dentist's new-patient guide, a restaurant's recipe for the dish people always ask about. Useful and specific beats generic "10% off your first email."
- **Event signups.** Workshops, tastings, open houses — anyone who registers is warm. Ask for email consent at registration.

### What not to do

Never buy a list. Never auto-subscribe everyone who ever emailed you or handed over a business card. Beyond being ineffective — these people ignore you at best and report you at worst — Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) prohibits sending a commercial electronic message without the recipient's consent, express or implied ([Justice Laws, s. 6](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/page-1.html)). Three rules cover most of what a local business does:

- **Implied consent runs on a clock.** An existing business relationship covers someone who bought from you within the past two years, or made an inquiry within the past six months ([s. 10](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/page-2.html)). After that it lapses. Use the window to ask for a proper yes.
- **Every commercial message says who sent it and how to stop it.** The Act requires sender identification, contact information and an unsubscribe mechanism in each one. The unsubscribe has to keep working for at least 60 days after you send, and a request must take effect within 10 business days (s. 11). Most email platforms add the footer; your job is to fill in real business details and honour opt-outs that reach you any other way, like a reply or a word at the counter.
- **It isn't only email.** CASL's definition of an "electronic address" includes instant messaging accounts, so the same rules follow you onto WeChat. Our [WeChat private traffic playbook](/blog/wechat-private-traffic-playbook/) covers how to ask for consent there.

We won't play lawyer on the edge cases (this is general information, not legal advice), but the principle is simple and worth internalizing: **if they didn't say yes, don't send.** A 400-person list of genuine opt-ins will outperform a 4,000-person list of conscripts every single time.

## The cadence that actually works

Monthly. Biweekly if you truly have that much worth saying. Not daily — daily is how businesses burn a list they spent a year building.

The format we recommend fits in one line: **one useful thing + one offer.** A seasonal tip plus a booking link. A neighbourhood update plus this month's special. The useful thing earns the open; the offer earns the revenue. Skip the useful thing and you're just a coupon machine people learn to delete.

One message worth automating: a thank-you after a visit or job, with a link to leave a Google review. Send it to every customer, not only the happy ones. Google's policy bars businesses from selectively soliciting positive reviews ([Google](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114)), and our [reviews guide](/blog/google-reviews-management-guide/) covers how to ask without breaking it.

## What to actually write about

The businesses that struggle with email are usually trying to write like a corporation. Don't. You're local — that's the entire advantage.

- **Behind the scenes.** New equipment, a staff milestone, how the thing actually gets made.
- **Neighbourhood news.** Street closures, local events, a shout-out to the business next door.
- **Seasonal tips tied to your service.** Gutter prep before the rain, skin care when the air turns dry, what to plant in March.
- **Early access.** Let subscribers book, buy, or reserve before the general public. This is the single best answer to "why should I sign up?"

## Bilingual email, done deliberately

If you serve Metro Vancouver, odds are part of your audience prefers Chinese. Two workable approaches: **separate lists** (a language choice at signup, then each list gets natively written emails) or a **single bilingual email** with a clear language toggle or stacked sections. Separate lists perform better because subject lines can be written natively per language — a translated subject line reads like one, and the subject line decides whether anything else gets read. It's the same trade-off bilingual brands weigh on social, which we walk through in [one account or two](/blog/bilingual-social-media-account-strategy/).

## Metrics that mean something

Open rates have been unreliable since privacy changes led mail clients to pre-load messages. Apple's Mail Privacy Protection, for one, downloads remote content (including the invisible pixel that counts an "open") in the background "regardless of whether you engage with the email" ([Apple](https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/mail-privacy-protection/)). Treat open rates as a rough trend line, not a scoreboard. What's real:

- **Clicks** — someone did something.
- **Replies** — someone talked to you. For a local business this is gold; invite it.
- **Redemptions** — the offer got used, in person. The metric that pays rent.

## Feed the newsletter with your blog

Every article you publish is a newsletter section you don't have to write from scratch: two sentences of summary plus a link. The blog builds long-term search visibility — including [your presence on Google](/blog/google-business-profile-guide-vancouver/) and, increasingly, in [AI recommendations](/blog/how-ai-assistants-recommend-local-businesses/) — while the newsletter puts each piece in front of the people most likely to act on it. One effort, two channels.
