Ask most business owners about Google reviews and they'll quote you one number: their star rating. But that's not how customers actually read your reviews. A 4.9 built on twelve reviews from 2023 loses to a 4.6 with fresh, detailed reviews arriving every week. Recency and substance carry the weight, and both are things you can influence this month without spending a dollar or breaking a single rule.

The rules did move, though. Google rewrote parts of its review policy in February 2026, and some tactics that used to be routine advice are now out. Here's the system we use under the current rules. (We've revised this guide accordingly: an earlier version suggested asking your happiest customers each week, which Google's policy treats as selectively soliciting positive reviews.)

## Recency and substance beat raw stars

Customers instinctively check the newest reviews first — they want to know what your business is like *now*, not two owners ago.

Google counts reviews too. Its help page on local ranking says "more reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking," and lists review count as part of prominence ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091)). Google doesn't publish how much recency matters, but practitioners rate review signals highly: in Whitespark's 2026 Local Search Ranking Factors survey of 47 local-search experts, reviews made up about 20% of what drives map-pack rankings ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/)). In our experience, a steady stream of recent reviews does more than a large but dormant pile.

Substance matters just as much. "Great place!" tells Google and future customers almost nothing. "The team repaired our furnace the same day and explained the invoice line by line" is a small piece of marketing copy you didn't have to write — full of the words people actually search for. You can't ask customers to write it that way (more on that below). You can make detailed reviews far more likely by asking at the right time.

## How to ask — the legitimate way

Most customers will leave a review if you ask at the right moment and make it effortless. The word that matters is *customers*, not *happy customers*: under Google's rules, everyone gets the same ask.

### Ask at the end of every job, not just the good ones
Build the request into the moment the service is complete: the job wraps, the order is picked up, the appointment ends. That's when the details are fresh, which is why reviews requested then tend to say more. Make it a routine step for every customer, not a reward for the ones who smiled most. A day later, the memory has faded and so has your response rate.

### Offer a QR code or short link, then step back
A QR code linking to your review form — on the counter, the invoice, the packaging — removes every step between "I'll leave a review" and actually doing it. Google's own tips suggest asking customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122)). Use the short review link from your Business Profile.

The line to watch is pressure. Google's 2026 policy says merchants should not require or pressure customers to leave reviews while they're on the premises. A sign at the counter is an offer. Handing someone a tablet and waiting while they type, or making a review the condition for anything, is pressure.

### Follow up, once
A brief message the next day works because it's personal and low-pressure: "Thanks for choosing us. If you have a minute, an honest Google review helps other customers know what to expect." Notice what it leaves out: no "if you were happy," no suggested wording, nothing in return. One follow-up, then stop — nagging turns goodwill into annoyance. If you already email customers, it fits naturally into the [follow-up email](/blog/email-marketing-for-local-businesses/) you send anyway.

### What's against the rules
Google's [review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) is blunt, and its 2026 update added two new lines. Merchants must not:

- **Offer incentives.** No payment, discounts, free goods or services in exchange for a review — even an honest one — and none for changing or removing a negative review.
- **Gate reviews.** No discouraging negative reviews and no selectively asking for positive ones. Sending a satisfaction survey first and only giving the review link to people who scored you highly is gating. So is asking only the regulars you know love you.
- **Pressure people on the premises** (new in 2026). Don't require or push customers to review while they're still in your shop, clinic or restaurant.
- **Request specific content** (new in 2026). Don't ask customers to mention a service, a staff member, your neighbourhood or a keyword, and don't have staff ask for it either. The policy specifically names merchants who have staff solicit reviews that include specific content.
- **Fake or buy reviews.** Purchased reviews, reviews from people who were never customers, and undisclosed reviews from staff or owners all violate the policy, and they're usually obvious to readers anyway.

Google enforces this. Beyond removing the reviews, it can stop a profile from receiving new reviews for a set period, unpublish existing reviews for a period, and show customers a warning that fake reviews were removed. It emails the owner before applying a restriction, and the owner can appeal ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/14114287)).

In Canada there's a legal layer as well. The Competition Bureau has warned that anyone who writes or permits reviews that give a false or misleading impression could be liable under the Competition Act, and that employees must disclose their connection to the business when they review it ([Competition Bureau](https://www.canada.ca/en/competition-bureau/news/2024/01/online-reviews-posted-by-employees-businesses-could-be-liable.html)). Its deceptive marketing digest also lists giving customers incentives to leave positive reviews under astroturfing ([Competition Bureau](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices-digest-volume-1)).

Ask everyone, reward no one, filter nothing, script nothing.

Regulated health professionals should check their college as well, because it can be stricter than Google. In BC, the college for chiropractors, massage therapists, naturopathic physicians and TCM practitioners now bars licensees from even asking patients for reviews; our [clinic marketing guide](/blog/clinic-marketing-guide-vancouver/) covers the details.

## Replying to positive reviews without sounding robotic

Reply to every positive review — it signals to reviewers, prospects, and Google that someone is home. The trap is the template. Ten identical "Thank you for your kind words!" replies read worse than silence, and Google's own tips warn against sending everyone the same thank-you and ask that replies stay conversational, not promotional.

The fix is simple: reference one specific thing from their review. They mentioned the patio? Mention the patio back. Two sentences, written like a person, signed off like a person. Thirty seconds per review is the entire cost.

## The negative review playbook

A negative review isn't a crisis; a badly handled one is. Prospective customers judge you far more on how you respond than on the complaint itself.

1. **Respond within a day or two.** Speed shows attentiveness. But never reply angry — if the review stings, draft it, walk away, and post after you've cooled off.
2. **Acknowledge the specifics.** "We're sorry the Saturday wait ran past thirty minutes" beats a generic apology, because it proves you actually read the complaint.
3. **Take it offline.** Offer a real name and a real contact method. The public reply shows willingness; the resolution happens in private. Don't reveal anything about the customer in public — Google's guidance is never to share a reviewer's private information.
4. **Never argue publicly.** Even when the reviewer is wrong, a defensive back-and-forth makes you look worse to every future reader. State your side once, factually and calmly, then stop.
5. **Flag genuine fakes.** If a review is spam, from a competitor, or from someone who was never a customer, flag it through your profile and reply publicly with a calm factual note in the meantime. Reserve flagging for real policy violations — not honest criticism you don't enjoy.

### If someone demands money to make the reviews stop
Review extortion has its own Google help page: a sudden burst of one- and two-star reviews, followed by a demand for money, goods or services to remove them. Google's advice is short: "Do not engage with or pay the malicious individuals." Paying invites repeat attempts and doesn't guarantee removal. Instead, report it through Google's merchant extortion form with screenshots of the demands (dates, times, sender details), links to the suspicious reviews, and when the spike started ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16404809)).

## Reviews feed your local SEO — and AI recommendations

Every detailed review adds searchable words to your profile: services, neighbourhoods, product names. When someone searches "emergency plumber East Vancouver," reviews that happen to mention those things can help your profile match the query. It's one input to the local ranking we covered in our [Google Business Profile guide](/blog/google-business-profile-guide-vancouver/), and our [local SEO guide](/blog/local-seo-guide-vancouver-small-business/) puts it in order with everything else. It's also why Google now bars asking for those words: they only count when they're the customer's own.

AI assistants are the newer reader. In a BrightLocal survey of 1,227 US consumers in July 2026, 31% said they use AI tools monthly to find local businesses, up from 6% a year earlier, and 75% of those AI users still checked Google or Maps before getting in touch ([eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/shoppers-use-ai-find-local-businesses--then-double-check-on-google)). So your reviews get read twice: once by the assistant assembling an answer, then by the customer double-checking it. ## Your weekly review-ops routine

This takes fifteen minutes a week:

- **Monday:** Read new reviews. Reply to every one — specific thanks for positives, the playbook for negatives.
- **Midweek:** Check that every job or visit this week got the same request — the QR code at the counter or one follow-up message. Every customer, not just the ones who seemed happiest.
- **Friday:** Check that your review QR code and short link still work, and skim the month's reviews for recurring themes — free operational feedback. Watch for a sudden spike of one-star reviews from unfamiliar accounts.

Small, boring, consistent. That's what a durable review base is built on.
