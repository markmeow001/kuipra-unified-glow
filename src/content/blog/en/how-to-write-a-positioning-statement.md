Ask a room of business owners to describe their positioning and you'll usually get a tagline, a mission statement, or a list of services. None of those are positioning. A positioning statement is one unglamorous internal sentence that answers a single question: **when a specific customer compares you to the alternatives, why do you win?**

We've written about why [strategy has to come before tactics](/blog/brand-strategy-vs-marketing-tactics/). The positioning statement is the first — and most compact — piece of that strategy. Here's how to write one that actually holds up.

## The formula

The classic structure has survived decades of marketing fashion because every blank forces a decision:

> **For [audience], [brand] is the [category] that [differentiator] because [proof].**

- **Audience** — a specific segment, not "everyone who drinks coffee."
- **Category** — the mental shelf customers already use. Fight the urge to invent a new one.
- **Differentiator** — one meaningful difference, stated as a benefit to the customer.
- **Proof** — the reason to believe. If you can't finish this clause honestly, the differentiator is a wish, not a position.

This sentence is internal. It's allowed to be clunky. Its job is to be *true and specific*, not pretty — the pretty versions (taglines, ads, bios) get written from it later.

## The three mistakes that ruin most drafts

### 1. Too broad

"For people who value quality" describes every business on the planet. If your audience clause could apply to your competitor across the street, you haven't chosen an audience — you've avoided choosing one. Narrow until it feels slightly uncomfortable. Positioning that excludes no one persuades no one.

### 2. Feature, not benefit

"The clinic that uses digital scanners" is a feature. Nobody wakes up wanting a scanner. The customer-facing version is what the feature *does for them*: fewer appointments, no gag-inducing impressions, a same-week fit. Translate every feature into the outcome it buys the customer.

### 3. Unprovable claims

"The best," "premium quality," "unmatched service" — these are claims that cost nothing to make, which is exactly why customers discount them to zero. If the proof clause can't cite something concrete — a process, a credential, a guarantee, a verifiable practice — cut the claim and find one you can back.

## Worked examples

These are generic archetypes — fictional composites of common Vancouver business types, not real companies or clients. Notice how each one makes a narrow choice and backs it with checkable proof.

### A specialty coffee roaster

> For East Van cafés and home brewers who care where their beans come from, **[Roaster]** is the small-batch roaster that lets you trace every bag to a single farm, because we publish the farm, lot, and roast date on every label and buy through direct-trade relationships.

The audience excludes the grab-and-go crowd on purpose. The proof is a practice anyone can verify by picking up a bag.

### A family dental clinic

> For young families near [neighbourhood] juggling multiple schedules, **[Clinic]** is the dental clinic that makes visits genuinely low-stress, because we book family-block appointments, offer evening and Saturday hours, and explain every treatment and cost before we start.

No "gentle, caring team" wallpaper — three concrete operational choices a parent can test on the first phone call.

### An event venue

> For couples and event planners who want a venue that doesn't feel like a hotel ballroom, **[Venue]** is the heritage event space that lets you shape the room to your event, because we offer an open vendor policy, a flexible floor plan, and a single point of contact from booking to teardown.

The category ("heritage event space") is honest — it's a shelf people already shop. The differentiator is a benefit (control), and the proof is policy, not adjectives.

## Pressure-test before you ship it

A draft isn't done until it survives four questions:

1. **The reversal test.** Could a competitor claim the opposite and still sound sane? "We use quality ingredients" fails — nobody claims the opposite. "We publish the farm on every label" passes.
2. **The swap test.** Put a competitor's name in your sentence. If it still reads fine, you've described the category, not your position.
3. **The receptionist test.** Can your newest employee say why customers pick you, in one sentence, without a script? If positioning lives only in a slide deck, it doesn't exist.
4. **The proof audit.** For every claim, name the evidence a skeptical customer could check. No evidence, no claim.

A statement that passes all four becomes the filter for everything downstream: content pillars, ad copy, even which opportunities to decline. That's the whole point — one sentence, decided once, used daily.
