Our [WeChat guide](/blog/wechat-marketing-canadian-business/) explained how the ecosystem works and what to prioritise. The question that usually comes next is more practical: *fine, but what do we actually do on Monday?*

This is that answer: the operating manual for what Chinese marketers call **私域流量**, or private traffic.

## What private traffic is, and why it matters here

Public traffic is the customers you reach through a platform that controls access: Google search, Xiaohongshu, Instagram. Every time you want those people again, you have to rank again, post again or pay again.

Private traffic is the people you can reach directly: your contacts and your groups. The idea is not new; an email list is private traffic. The difference is where it lives. For many of the Chinese-speaking customers we described in our [guide to Vancouver's Chinese community](/blog/marketing-to-vancouver-chinese-community/), the inbox they actually check is WeChat, not email.

The two work as a pair. Public platforms bring new people in, which is what our [Xiaohongshu guide](/blog/xiaohongshu-marketing-vancouver/) is about. Private traffic is what you do with them afterwards, so that the second, third and tenth purchase doesn't cost you another round of ads.

## Step 1: Put the relationship on an account the business owns

The most common failure is quiet. A front-desk employee adds customers from their own phone because it is convenient. Two years later they leave, and eight hundred customer relationships leave with them.

Fix this before you add a single contact:

- **A dedicated phone and WeChat account** that belongs to the business, used by whoever is on shift.
- **A clear name and photo**: the business name plus a role, such as "[Business] · Front Desk", with your logo or storefront as the profile picture.
- **Some Moments posts before you start.** The first thing many new contacts do is scroll your Moments to see who you are. An empty feed reads as a sales account.

## Step 2: Build the entry points, and give people a reason to add you

"Add us on WeChat" is not a reason. People add an account when it does something for them:

- **Order and pickup updates**: "We'll message you when it's ready."
- **Booking changes**: easier than phone tag for clinics, salons and tutors.
- **First access**: a members' group that hears about limited items or seasonal boxes before anyone else.
- **Aftercare**: a place to ask the follow-up question they would otherwise not bother calling about.

Then put the entry points where the decision happens:

- **At the counter or reception**, on a small printed card with the account's own QR code. **Never print a group QR code.** According to the [WeChat Help Center](https://cs.help.wechat.com/hc/en-us/articles/11917769362191-How-long-is-a-group-QR-Code-valid-for), a group QR code is valid for seven days, and it stops working immediately if the person who shared it leaves the group. A printed group code is dead within a week.
- **In the bag, box or receipt**, with the same reason written next to it.
- **On your website's contact page**, next to your phone number and email.

Give staff one sentence to say at checkout, and make it about the customer: *"If you add our WeChat, we'll message you when your order's ready, and you'll hear about the Lunar New Year boxes first."*

## Step 3: Tag every contact on the day they add you

WeChat lets you add a remark and tags to every contact. Use them from day one, because they are almost impossible to reconstruct once you have two thousand contacts.

A simple set of tags is enough:

- **Source**: in-store, Xiaohongshu, referral, event.
- **Interest**: what they bought or asked about.
- **Language**: Mandarin, Cantonese or English, so you reply the way they prefer.
- **Consent**: whether they agreed to receive offers, and the date. More on why in the Canadian section below.

Tags pay off twice. You can send relevant messages instead of blasting everyone, and you can limit who sees a Moments post, so your catering offer reaches office managers rather than every diner who ever ordered noodles.

## Step 4: Run groups with one purpose each

Groups are where private traffic becomes repeat business, and also where it most often dies. A few rules keep them alive.

**One group, one job.** An order group for a bakery's weekend pre-orders. A members' group for first access. A cohort group for a class. A group for everything soon becomes a group for nothing, and people mute it.

**Rules in the first message.** What the group is for, roughly how often you post, no advertising from other members, and how to leave. Pin it. Good rules keep the group useful for the people in it.

**A host who answers.** Silence kills groups faster than low membership does. Someone needs to reply during business hours and keep conversation from drifting into spam.

**Several smaller groups, not one giant one.** WeChat groups can hold up to 500 members, but the [WeChat Help Center](https://cs.help.wechat.com/hc/en-us/articles/11917722718351-Add-friends-into-a-group-chat) notes that past 40 members new people get an invitation instead of being added directly, and past 100 they must verify their identity. Each step adds friction. Splitting by purpose or neighbourhood keeps groups easy to join and relevant to the people in them.

**A predictable rhythm.** "Orders open Tuesday, close Thursday, pickup Saturday" beats random posts. Members learn when to pay attention, and your kitchen or calendar can plan around it. Use poster-style images with the details designed in; they survive being forwarded to someone else's group.

## Step 5: Use Moments as your low-pressure channel

Moments is not pushed into anyone's chat list; people browse it when they choose to. That makes it the right place for frequent, low-pressure posts: new arrivals, work you finished, the team behind the counter, a useful tip.

Most posts should not be offers. Your Moments feed builds the trust that makes a direct message welcome when you do send one. Use tag-based visibility so each post reaches the people it is relevant to.

## Step 6: Turn contacts into repeat customers

A contact list is only an asset if it brings people back. The moves that matter:

- **A follow-up after the first purchase**, a few days later, that is useful rather than salesy: how to store it, how to use it, what to expect next.
- **Timely reminders based on tags**, so someone hears from you when they are likely to need you again.
- **Festival moments planned in advance.** Lunar New Year and Mid-Autumn offers run through groups extremely well, but only if they are planned weeks ahead. Our [seasonal marketing calendar](/blog/vancouver-seasonal-marketing-calendar/) has the dates.
- **Easy forwarding.** WeChat runs on relationships. A clear poster a member can forward to their family group is worth more than any ad.

## The Canadian part: consent

Most WeChat playbooks are written for the mainland Chinese market. A Canadian business has one more rule to follow.

Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation defines an "electronic address" to include an instant messaging account ([Justice Laws, s. 1](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/page-1.html)). A promotional message sent one-to-one to a WeChat contact is therefore in the same category as a promotional email, which we covered in our [email marketing guide](/blog/email-marketing-for-local-businesses/). In practice:

- **Implied consent has time limits.** Under the Act, an existing business relationship covers someone who bought from you within the past two years, or made an inquiry within the past six months ([s. 10](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/page-2.html)). Outside those windows, you need a yes.
- **Ask at the moment they add you.** "Is it okay if we also send you offers here?" Asked right then, it feels like part of the conversation rather than a form. Record the answer and the date in their contact remark. That is what the consent tag in Step 3 is for.
- **Make every promotional message identifiable and easy to stop.** Say who it is from and how to opt out, and honour the request.

This is general information, not legal advice. For edge cases, such as contacts someone else gave you or promotions posted into groups you do not run, ask a lawyer.

## What to track

A one-page weekly sheet is enough:

- **New contacts by source**: which entry point is actually working.
- **Orders and bookings that came through WeChat**: from a chat, a group post or a Moments post.
- **Repeat rate**: how often WeChat contacts come back compared with everyone else.
- **Leaves and opt-outs**: if this number climbs, you are posting too often or too far off-topic.

## Private traffic compounds

None of this works like a campaign. It works like a savings account: small and slow for the first few months, then increasingly valuable, because every customer you keep is one you do not have to win again.
